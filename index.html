The Michelson–Morley Experiment Sec. 1.1

The experiment that found nothing, and by finding nothing forced everything else on this page. Two light beams race down perpendicular arms; if the earth moves through a light-carrying ether, the beams should return out of step.

t∥ = (2L/c)/(1−β²)    t⊥ = (2L/c)/√(1−β²)   —   fringe shift on 90° rotation n = 2cΔt/λ

What you're looking at

Light from a source hits a half-silvered mirror that splits it in two. One beam runs along the direction of the supposed ether wind and back; the other runs across it and back. They recombine at the screen, and any difference in transit time shows up as a shift in the interference fringes. The lower panel shows those fringes, with the pattern the ether theory predicts once you rotate the whole apparatus by 90° and swap which arm faces the wind.

The asymmetry the experiment hunts for is a familiar one. A swimmer crossing a river and returning takes less time than one swimming the same distance upstream and back, because the upstream leg is punished more than the downstream leg rewards. Light in an ether should behave the same way, and the two transit times printed on the diagram differ by exactly that amount.

Try this

The 1887 configuration was an effective arm length of about 11 m, sodium light at 589 nm, and the earth's orbital speed of 30 km/s. That predicts a shift of roughly 0.37 fringes, while the apparatus could comfortably resolve 0.01 — the effect should have been about forty times larger than the noise. Michelson and Morley were not straining to see something marginal; they were looking for something that could hardly be missed. Now drag the ether wind down toward zero and watch the predicted shift collapse as β². That quadratic dependence is why the experiment needed such long arms in the first place.

What the null result cost

Nothing moved. Not in 1887, and not in any of the steadily more sensitive repetitions since. The result is arguably the most consequential non-observation in the history of physics, and it left two conclusions standing. There is no ether, so there is no universal frame against which absolute motion could be measured — all motion is relative to some specified frame. And the speed of light comes out the same regardless of how the observer moves, which is exactly what waves in a medium never do.

The second of those becomes Einstein's second postulate, and once you accept it the rest of this chapter is forced. If c is the same for everyone, then the light clock in the next card must run slow when it moves, lengths must contract, and simultaneity must become frame-dependent. None of that was invented to explain the null result; it is what follows from taking it seriously.

The catch

Notice the shape of the β² dependence: the predicted effect is second order in v/c, not first. A first-order effect would have been detected decades earlier by much cruder apparatus. That is precisely why FitzGerald and Lorentz could rescue the ether for a while by proposing that objects physically contract along their direction of motion by exactly the factor needed to cancel the difference — the contraction in the length-contraction card below, arrived at for entirely the wrong reason. Their fix worked numerically but explained nothing. Einstein's contribution was not a better fix; it was noticing that no fix was needed once you stopped assuming space and time were absolute.

Light-Clock Time Dilation Eq. 1.3

A photon bounces between two mirrors L₀ apart. Watch the same clock tick at rest, and again as seen from the ground while it flies past at speed v.

Δt = Δt₀ / √(1−β²) = γΔt₀,   β=v/c,   γ=1/√(1−β²)

What you're looking at

A light clock is the simplest possible clock: a photon bouncing between two mirrors a fixed distance L₀ apart. One round trip is one tick, so the period at rest is Δt₀ = 2L₀/c. The top lane shows that clock sitting still. The bottom lane shows the same clock, built identically, flying past you at βc — the faint trail traces the photon's actual path through your frame.

Notice what the trail is doing: while the photon travels from one mirror to the other, the whole clock has also moved sideways, so the photon's path is a diagonal, not a vertical line. It has further to go. And here is where the second postulate bites — the photon cannot compensate by moving faster, because light travels at c for every observer, no matter how the source moves. Longer path, same speed, therefore longer tick. Watch the two tick counters in the readout drift apart.

Try this

Set β = 0.866. That gives γ = 2 exactly, and the moving clock ticks precisely half as often — let it run until the rest clock reaches 10 ticks and you should find the moving one at 5. Then push β toward 0.995 and watch the moving clock nearly freeze. Coming back the other way, at β = 0.1 the two lanes look essentially identical: γ = 1.005, a half-percent effect. That is exactly why nobody noticed any of this until we could accelerate particles.

Why this isn't a trick of the apparatus

It is tempting to think the moving light clock is merely malfunctioning while "real" time ticks on regardless. But nothing here is specific to photons and mirrors. If the moving clock read slow while a wristwatch beside it kept true time, you could compare the two and discover your own absolute speed through space — and the first postulate forbids that. Every process in the moving frame must slow by the same factor, which is another way of saying it is time itself, not any particular clock, that runs slow.

Beiser's demonstration of this is the muon. Muons created by cosmic rays high in the atmosphere decay after an average of just 2.2 µs in their own rest frame, which at their speed of 0.998c would carry them only 0.66 km — yet they are created 6 km up and reach sea level in profusion, roughly one per square centimetre per minute. In our frame their lifetime is stretched by γ ≈ 16 to 34.8 µs, and in that time they cover 10.4 km. The particles arrive because time dilation is real, and biological, chemical and nuclear clocks all slow together.

The catch that makes it consistent

The effect is perfectly symmetric: an observer riding the bottom clock sees your clock running slow by the same factor γ, with equal justification. That sounds like a flat contradiction, and resolving it is the whole point of the Minkowski diagram in the last card on this page — the two of you also disagree about which distant events happen "at the same time," and that disagreement is exactly the right size to reconcile the two accounts.

Length Contraction Eq. 1.9

A rod of proper length L₀ is laid along the direction of motion. Only the along-motion dimension shrinks.

L = L₀√(1−β²)

What you're looking at

The red bar is the rod measured in its own rest frame — its proper length L₀, the longest any observer will ever find it to be. The teal bar is the length you measure from the ground as it flies past at βc. The curve underneath plots the whole story at once: L/L₀ against β, with the dot marking where your current setting sits on it.

The shape of that curve is the thing worth absorbing. It leaves 1.0 almost reluctantly — it is flat near the origin, so at ordinary speeds the contraction is unmeasurably small — and then falls off a cliff as β approaches 1. Contraction is not a gentle effect that grows steadily with speed; it is a nothing-nothing-nothing-everything effect concentrated in the last few percent before c.

Try this

Start at β = 0.1 — a rod moving at 30,000 km/s, which is fast beyond any human experience, and it shrinks by half a percent. Now β = 0.6 gives 80% of proper length, β = 0.9 gives 44%, and β = 0.999 leaves you with 4.5%. The last step in that sequence covers less than a tenth as much speed as the first, and does more than fifty times as much contracting.

The same fact, told from the other frame

Length contraction and time dilation are not two independent phenomena; they are one phenomenon described from two frames, and Beiser derives the second from the first for exactly that reason. Return to the muon from the card above. In our frame, the muon survives the 6 km journey because its lifetime is dilated to 34.8 µs. But ride along with the muon, and its lifetime is a perfectly ordinary 2.2 µs — nothing is stretched at all.

What saves the muon in its own frame is that the atmosphere is the thing in motion, rushing up at 0.998c and contracted by the same factor of 16. The 6 km of air we measure is only about 0.4 km to the muon, and a 2.2 µs lifetime is ample to cross it. Both observers agree the muon reaches the ground — they simply disagree about whether it was the clock or the distance that did the accommodating.

The catch

"Length" here means what you get by marking the positions of both ends simultaneously in your frame and subtracting — and since observers disagree about simultaneity, they inevitably disagree about length. This is a statement about measurement, not about appearance: a fast rod does not look compressed to the eye or to a camera, because light from its far end left earlier than light from its near end, which produces a quite different (and merely optical) distortion. Note too that only the along-motion dimension changes. A rod held perpendicular to the motion is unaffected, which is why the light clock's mirror spacing L₀ was safe to treat as unchanged.

Relativistic Velocity Addition Appendix I, Eq. 1.49

A spacecraft S′ moves at speed v relative to the earth S. Something moves at u′ inside S′, in the same direction. What speed does earth measure?

u = (u′ + v) / (1 + u′v/c²)   —   classically u = u′ + v

What you're looking at

A single velocity axis running from −2c to +2c, with the regions beyond ±c shaded red because nothing with mass may be found there. Three markers sit on it: the grey one is the frame's own speed v, the upper marker is the naive classical sum u′ + v, and the teal one below is the correct relativistic result. Drag either slider and watch the classical marker wander cheerfully into forbidden territory while the teal marker refuses to leave the allowed zone, no matter what you do to it.

The entire difference is the denominator, 1 + u′v/c². At everyday speeds that term is so close to 1 that Galileo's answer is right to more decimal places than anyone can measure — for a jet at 300 m/s firing a bullet at 1000 m/s, the correction shows up around the twelfth decimal place. The formula does not replace classical velocity addition so much as reveal it as a low-speed approximation that happened to be all anyone could test for three centuries.

Try this

Reproduce the book's worked example first: v = 0.90, u′ = 0.50. Classically that is 1.40c, comfortably illegal; relativistically it is 0.966c. Now push both sliders to their 0.99 extremes — classically 1.98c, relativistically 0.99995c. This is the pattern to notice: feeding the formula speeds arbitrarily close to c gives back something closer still to c, but never past it. Then set u′ = 0.99 and sweep v across its whole range; the teal marker barely stirs. A beam that is nearly light-speed in one frame is nearly light-speed in all of them, which is the second postulate re-emerging as an algebraic consequence rather than an assumption.

Where it comes from

This is not a patch bolted on to rescue the speed limit — it drops straight out of the Lorentz transformation. Write down how S and S′ disagree about both position and time, form the ratio dx/dt that each calls "velocity," and the denominator appears on its own. That shared origin is why the same factor keeps turning up: the reason velocities do not simply add is the same reason lengths contract and clocks dilate, namely that a boost mixes space and time into each other instead of shifting space alone.

The catch

Give the object a negative u′ while v is positive and you can make the two contributions fight, sending the result back toward zero or negative — the formula handles subtraction with no special cases. But notice it never lets the total escape the interval (−c, c) from inside, in either direction. Light itself is the boundary case: put u′ = c into the algebra and you get exactly c back for any v, which is the only fixed point of the transformation.

Relativistic Doppler Effect & Cosmological Redshift Eq. 1.8, Hubble's law

Purely radial motion (source and observer on the same line). Positive β = receding (redshift); negative = approaching (blueshift).

ν = ν₀ √[(1−β)/(1+β)]   |   Hubble: β = v/c = H₀d/c,  H₀≈21 km/s per 10⁶ ly
source colour → observed colour

What you're looking at

The strip is the visible spectrum from 380 to 750 nm. The solid black line marks the wavelength the source actually emits, λ₀; the dashed red line marks what arrives at your detector. The two swatches beside the controls show the same comparison as flat colour, which makes small shifts easier to judge than a line on a rainbow does. Positive β means the source is receding and the dashed line walks to the right (redshift); negative β means approaching, and it walks left (blueshift).

The relativistic Doppler formula differs from the acoustic one you already know in two ways. First, sound needs a medium, so it matters separately whether the source moves through the air or the listener does — those give measurably different answers. Light has no medium, so only the relative velocity can appear in the formula, and it does. Second, the moving source's own emission process is time-dilated, which contributes an extra factor of γ that has no acoustic counterpart at all.

Try this

Work Beiser's Example 1.3: a galaxy in Hydra recedes at 6.12 × 10⁷ m/s, which is β = 0.204, and emits a green line at λ₀ = 500 nm. Dial those in and the observed wavelength lands near 615 nm — green light arriving as orange, a shift of 23%. Then try Example 1.2, the driver who told the judge the red light looked green. Red is 480 THz and green is 560 THz, which needs β ≈ −0.155 — about 46,000 km/s. The judge, fining $1 for each km/h over the 80 km/h limit, charged her roughly $165 million.

From a colour shift to an expanding universe

The second slider row reframes the same physics cosmologically. Hubble's law says recession speed grows in proportion to distance, at roughly 21 km/s for every million light-years, so a galaxy's redshift doubles as a distance measurement. Drag the distance slider and watch β follow it — the deduction that the universe is expanding rests on precisely this, applied to spectral lines whose rest wavelengths we know from laboratory atoms.

One subtlety worth flagging: for the genuinely distant universe, the redshift is better understood as space itself expanding while the light is in flight than as motion through space, and the simple formula here is the nearby-galaxy approximation. Within a few hundred million light-years, which is the range of this slider, the two descriptions agree closely.

The catch

This formula is for purely radial motion, source and observer on the same line. A source moving perpendicular to your line of sight is not exempt from the effect — it still shows a redshift, purely from time dilation, with no classical counterpart whatsoever. That transverse Doppler shift is a second-order effect (it depends on β² rather than β), which is why it went unobserved for so long, and why its eventual measurement was such a clean confirmation of time dilation on its own.

Twin Paradox Sec. 1.5

Dick flies at βc to a star D light-years away and back; Jane stays on Earth. The spacetime diagram is drawn in the Earth frame (x in ly, ct in years).

T₀(traveler) = (2D/β)√(1−β²)     T(earth) = 2D/β

What you're looking at

A spacetime diagram drawn in Jane's (Earth's) frame: distance runs horizontally in light-years, time runs vertically in years. Jane never moves, so her worldline is the vertical teal line straight up the time axis. Dick's red worldline goes out at a slope set by β, turns a sharp corner at the star, and comes back. The dashed grey 45° line is the path a light signal would take — every physical worldline must stay steeper than it.

Both twins start at the same event (bottom corner) and meet again at the same event (top of Jane's line). There is no ambiguity about who is older when they are standing next to each other, which is what makes this a real prediction rather than a matter of perspective. The labels on the diagram give each twin's accumulated age at the turnaround and at the reunion.

Try this

Set the book's scenario exactly: β = 0.80, D = 20 light-years, starting age 20. Jane waits 50 years and is 70 at the reunion; Dick experiences only 30 and is 50. As Beiser puts it, Jane sees Dick's heart beat 3 times for every 5 of hers, and thinks 3 thoughts for every 5 of hers. Now sweep β up to 0.99 with the distance fixed: the trip gets a little quicker for Jane but dramatically quicker for Dick, and the age gap widens toward the extreme. Push β down to 0.1 instead and the two ages nearly converge — a slow traveller barely gains anything.

Where the paradox actually dissolves

The puzzle is usually stated like this: motion is relative, so from Dick's point of view it is Jane who is moving, and she should end up younger. Both cannot be right. The resolution is that the two situations are not mirror images. Jane remains in a single inertial frame for the whole story. Dick does not — he changes frames when he departs, again when he turns around, and again when he lands. That corner in the red worldline has no counterpart in Jane's straight one, and it is what breaks the symmetry.

Dick's own accounting agrees, without invoking time dilation for himself at all. In his frame the distance to the star is length-contracted from 20 light-years to 12, and at 0.8c that takes 15 years each way — 30 years total, exactly what the diagram reports. The two twins explain the result with different mechanisms and arrive at the identical number, which is the signature of a theory that hangs together.

There is an elegant way to see the whole thing at a glance. In ordinary geometry, a straight line is the shortest path between two points. In the geometry of spacetime the minus sign in the interval flips this: between two events, the straight (unaccelerated) worldline carries the longest proper time. Dick's bent path is therefore necessarily the shorter experience. Ageing less is simply the price of taking a detour through spacetime.

The catch

Nothing strange happens to either twin during the trip — neither one ever catches their own clock misbehaving. If each sends a radio signal once a year, Beiser's Example 1.4 works out that Dick receives 5 signals on the way out (arriving 3 years apart) and 45 on the way back (arriving 4 months apart), for 50 in total; Jane receives 30. Each twin receives exactly as many signals as the other twin had years. The asymmetry is not hidden in the physics — it is right there in the tally.

Relativistic vs Classical Kinetic Energy & Momentum Eq. 1.17, 1.20

Classical mechanics is the β→0 limit — watch where it quietly stops being a safe approximation.

KE = (γ−1)mc²  vs  ½mυ²   |   p = γmυ  vs  mυ

What you're looking at

Two plots against β, both in units that remove the particle's mass from the picture: kinetic energy in multiples of its rest energy mc² on the left, momentum in multiples of mc on the right. In each, the grey dashed curve is what classical mechanics predicts and the solid coloured curve is the relativistic truth. The dots mark your current β on both. The particle selector then converts those dimensionless numbers into MeV in the readout, so you can see what the same β costs for an electron versus a proton.

Look at how the two curves separate. Near the origin they are indistinguishable — the relativistic expression's leading term is ½mυ², so classical mechanics is not merely close, it is the first term of the correct answer. Classical kinetic energy stays within 1% of the truth all the way up to about 0.12c, roughly 36,000 km/s. Beyond that the curves peel apart and the relativistic one turns sharply upward, eventually leaving the top of the frame entirely.

Try this

Step through β = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99 and watch the "understatement" line in the readout: the classical formula is low by about 0.75%, then 19%, then 69%, then 92%. At 0.99c, classical mechanics gets the energy wrong by more than a factor of twelve. Now leave β at 0.99 and switch particles: the same β costs an electron about 3.1 MeV and a proton about 5.7 GeV, because the identical γ multiplies very different rest energies.

Why c is a speed limit rather than a speed record

The momentum plot is the more revealing of the two. Classically, momentum is proportional to speed, so doubling a particle's momentum doubles its speed and there is no reason the process should ever stop. Relativistically, p = γmυ has γ growing without bound as β → 1, so momentum runs to infinity while speed merely creeps toward c. At β = 0.999 the momentum is already 22 times mc while the speed is still short of the target.

That is what makes c unreachable for anything with mass. It is not that some barrier appears at c; it is that each additional increment of speed costs more energy than the last, without limit. Accelerator physics is the daily confirmation — machines are specified by the energy they deliver, not the speed they reach, because past a certain point every extra GeV buys another decimal place of 0.999... and essentially no extra velocity.

The catch

Beiser presents these results in the older language of "relativistic mass" increasing with speed, which is how the γ was often absorbed historically. Modern practice keeps mass as a fixed, frame-independent property and puts the γ explicitly into the momentum and energy, which is the convention used here. The physics is identical either way — but the modern phrasing makes it clearer that the useful master equation is E² = (mc²)² + (pc)², which holds for everything, and which reduces to E = pc for a massless particle. That limit is exactly what the photon momentum in the Compton card of the next chapter relies on.

Minkowski Spacetime Diagram Appendix II

The x′, ct′ axes of a boosted frame S′ are not perpendicular to each other in the S diagram — that skew is the relativity of simultaneity. Dashed lines are lines of constant t′ (simultaneous events in S′).

ct′ axis: x = β(ct)    x′ axis: ct = βx    light cone: x = ±ct

What you're looking at

The black horizontal and vertical lines are the x and ct axes of your frame S. The dashed grey diagonals at 45° are the light cone. The red and teal lines are the axes of a frame S′ moving at β relative to you: the red line is everywhere that S′ calls "t′ = 0," and the teal line is everywhere it calls "x′ = 0," meaning the worldline of its own origin. The faint red dashed lines parallel to the red axis are other instants of S′ time — each one is a set of events that S′ insists happened simultaneously.

Drag β and watch the two coloured axes scissor toward each other, closing on the 45° light cone from both sides but never crossing it. At β = 0 they sit exactly on your own axes. Flip the sign of β and they scissor the other way. Notice that the light cone itself never moves — it is the one feature of the diagram every observer agrees on, which is just the second postulate drawn as a picture.

Try this

Set β to about 0.5 and follow one of the dashed red lines with your eye from left to right. Every event on it is simultaneous as far as S′ is concerned — yet the line is tilted, so in your frame those events sit at visibly different heights, meaning different times. Two events that are simultaneous for one observer are simply not simultaneous for the other, and the disagreement grows with β. Now push β to 0.95 and watch the tilt become extreme.

This is the card that explains the other six

Every apparent paradox earlier on this page traces back to this one picture. Length contraction depended on measuring both ends of a rod "at the same time," and the dashed lines show that the two frames mean different things by that phrase — so of course they get different lengths. The mutual time dilation that looked self-contradictory in the light-clock card is fine for the same reason: each observer is comparing a single moving clock against a whole row of their own clocks, and the two rows are not synchronised with each other.

Put slightly differently: a boost does not slide space along while time ticks on unchanged, which is what Galileo assumed. It rotates the axes of spacetime into one another, so that some of what you call duration becomes what S′ calls separation and vice versa. Time dilation, length contraction and velocity addition are three shadows cast by that one rotation.

The catch

Simultaneity being negotiable might look like an invitation to reorder cause and effect — but the axes can never rotate past 45°, however hard you push β. If one event can influence another, their separation is timelike, meaning the line joining them is steeper than the light cone, and no boost can flip which came first. Observers can disagree about the timing and ordering of events that are too far apart to affect each other, and about nothing else. Relativity gives up absolute simultaneity precisely so that it can keep causality.

Blackbody Radiation: Planck vs. Rayleigh–Jeans Eq. 2.3, 2.4

Classical physics (equipartition) predicts the energy density keeps climbing at short wavelengths — the ultraviolet catastrophe. Planck's quantization formula turns it over.

u(λ)dλ = (8πhc/λ⁵) · 1/(ehc/λkT−1) dλ   |   RJ: u(λ)dλ = 8πkT/λ⁴ dλ

What you're looking at

Energy density inside a heated cavity, plotted against wavelength. The solid red curve is Planck's formula and matches what is actually measured. The grey dashed curve is the Rayleigh–Jeans prediction — everything classical physics has to say on the subject. The pale blue band marks the visible range, and the teal dashed line marks the peak located by Wien's displacement law, λpeakT = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K.

Follow the grey curve from right to left. Out at long wavelengths it lies right on top of Planck's — classical physics is not wrong everywhere, and that is what made the failure so puzzling. But as the wavelength shortens the grey curve keeps climbing, and climbing, without limit. It predicts that a merely warm cavity should be pouring out unbounded energy at the ultraviolet end, and that opening the door of any oven should be instantly fatal. This is the ultraviolet catastrophe.

Where the classical argument goes wrong

The classical derivation has two ingredients. Count the standing-wave modes that fit in the cavity, which gives a number growing as ν² — that part is correct. Then assign each mode an average energy of kT, from the equipartition theorem, which was one of the most secure results in nineteenth-century physics. Multiply, and you get a spectrum rising as ν² forever.

Planck's fix was to allow a mode of frequency ν to hold energy only in whole multiples of hν. A high-frequency mode therefore cannot be excited a little; it must take a whole quantum or nothing. When hν grows large compared with the thermal energy kT available, the Boltzmann factor makes that all-or-nothing purchase overwhelmingly unlikely, so those modes sit unexcited and the curve turns over instead of diverging. Equipartition survives only where the quanta are small compared with kT — which is exactly the long-wavelength region where the two curves agree.

Try this

Set T = 5800 K, the Sun's surface: the peak lands near 500 nm, squarely inside the visible band. Our eyes are tuned to the output of the star we evolved under, which is less of a coincidence than it first appears. Now try T = 3000 K, a tungsten filament bulb — the peak is at about 970 nm, in the infrared, meaning most of what an incandescent bulb emits is heat you cannot see. Drop to T = 1500 K to recreate the comparison in Beiser's Fig. 2.8, and push to T = 10,000 K to send the peak into the ultraviolet. Toggle the overlay off and on at each temperature and watch where the grey curve departs from reality.

Why this card matters more than it looks

Planck introduced quantization in 1900 as what he later called "lucky guesswork" — a mathematical device to fit the measured curve, which he expected would eventually be explained away by classical means. It never was. Every other card in this chapter is a consequence of taking that device seriously: the constant h fixed here to match a glowing cavity is the same h that turns up in the photoelectric slope, in Compton's wavelength shift, and eventually in the hydrogen atom. This curve is where quantum physics starts.

Photoelectric Effect Eq. 2.8, 2.9

Einstein's equation: increasing intensity makes more photoelectrons, not faster ones. Only frequency sets the maximum kinetic energy.

KEmax = hν − φ = h(ν−ν₀)     stopping voltage V₀ = KEmax/e

What you're looking at

On the left is the experiment Beiser describes in Fig. 2.9: light strikes a metal plate, electrons come off, and a retarding voltage is turned up until even the fastest of them can no longer reach the collector. The three red curves are three different light intensities. Brighter light produces proportionally more current — the curves stack vertically — but look where they all hit zero. They stop at the same voltage V₀. Tripling the brightness does not give a single electron any extra energy.

On the right, KEmax is plotted against frequency for the selected metal, with two reference metals dashed in grey. These lines are parallel. Changing the metal slides the line sideways, changing the threshold frequency, but never tilts it — and the slope is Planck's constant h. This is the plot Millikan spent a decade measuring, partly in an effort to prove Einstein wrong; it gave instead the most precise value of h then available.

Why the wave picture cannot survive this

Classically, light energy arrives spread across a wavefront, and an electron soaks it up gradually like a pebble worked loose by water waves. Three things then follow, and all three are wrong. There should be a measurable delay while a dim source delivers enough energy — none is observed, to better than a nanosecond. Brighter light should eject faster electrons — it does not, as the left plot shows. And light of any frequency should work if you wait long enough or shine hard enough — yet below a threshold frequency, absolutely nothing happens.

Einstein's resolution was to take Planck's quantum literally: the energy is not merely emitted in packets, it travels in packets. One photon of energy hν is absorbed by one electron, all at once. The electron must spend φ getting out of the metal and keeps the rest. That single sentence explains all three observations at once — no delay because the transaction is instantaneous, no intensity dependence because more photons means more electrons rather than better-fed ones, and a threshold because a photon carrying less than φ cannot pay the toll no matter how many of its colleagues arrive.

Try this

Keep sodium (φ = 2.3 eV) and set λ = 300 nm: each photon carries 4.13 eV, the electron surrenders 2.3 eV to escape, and KEmax is 1.83 eV. Now drag the wavelength upward and watch KEmax fall to zero at about 539 nm — green light. Past that point, sodium emits nothing at all, and no amount of intensity will change it. Then switch to platinum (6.4 eV), whose threshold is down at 194 nm in the deep ultraviolet, and to cesium (1.9 eV), which still responds to red light at 652 nm. On the right-hand plot, watch the line shift sideways while its slope stays locked.

The catch

Notice what the threshold is set by: the frequency of the light, never its brightness. This is the sharpest possible break with the wave picture, because in wave physics intensity is the thing that carries energy and frequency is merely a label. It is also why Beiser calls x-ray production the inverse photoelectric effect — run the same transaction backwards, with a fast electron giving up its kinetic energy to create a photon, which is exactly what the next card does.

X-ray Production: the Inverse Photoelectric Effect Sec. 2.5

Run the photoelectric transaction backwards. Fast electrons slam into a metal target and their kinetic energy reappears as photons — and the spectrum stops dead at a shortest wavelength that classical physics has no way to account for.

λmin = hc/eV   —   set by the accelerating voltage alone, whatever the target is made of

What you're looking at

The x-ray spectrum coming off the target. The broad red hump is bremsstrahlung — "braking radiation" — produced as electrons decelerate in the target, and classical electromagnetism predicts it happily enough. What classical physics cannot produce is the wall on the left: the spectrum stops abruptly at λmin and there is nothing whatsoever beyond it. The grey dashed curve shows what the classical account expects instead, a continuum that simply keeps going.

The black spikes are characteristic lines, emitted when an incoming electron knocks out an inner-shell electron of the target atom and an outer electron drops in to fill the hole. They belong to the element, not to the machine.

Try this

Start with tungsten at 50 kV, which reproduces one of the curves in Beiser's Fig. 2.16: the cutoff sits at 24.8 pm. Now drag the voltage and watch the cutoff sweep left as 1/V while the continuum's peak stays pinned at twice λmin. Double the voltage and you exactly halve the cutoff wavelength. Next, recreate Fig. 2.17 by putting both targets at 35 kV: molybdenum shows its Kα and Kβ spikes at 71 and 63 pm, while tungsten shows none at all, because 35 kV cannot reach tungsten's 69.5 kV K edge. Push the voltage past 70 kV and tungsten's lines appear — at 20.9 pm, where they always were.

Two different things, two different rules

The contrast between those two features is the whole lesson of the card. The cutoff depends only on the accelerating voltage and not at all on what the target is made of. The characteristic lines depend only on the target element and not at all on the voltage — raising the voltage makes them brighter and can switch them on, but never moves them. One number belongs to the electron gun, the other to the atom.

The cutoff is the photoelectric equation run backwards. An electron accelerated through V arrives with kinetic energy eV, and the most it can possibly give to a single photon is all of it: hνmax = eV, hence λmin = hc/eV. A continuous, classical energy transfer would allow any amount to go into radiation with no upper bound on frequency. The existence of a sharp limit says the energy is handed over in one indivisible lump — the same claim Einstein made about absorption, now demonstrated in emission.

The catch

Measuring λmin against V gives h directly, independently of the photoelectric effect and of blackbody radiation — the relation is known as the Duane–Hunt law, and it agrees with the other two determinations. That agreement is what makes h a property of nature rather than a fudge factor: three experiments with nothing in common but the constant return the same number. Note also that most of the electrons' energy never becomes x-rays at all; the overwhelming majority is dissipated as heat, which is why real x-ray tubes need aggressive cooling.

Compton Scattering Eq. 2.21–2.23

A photon scatters off a free electron and comes out with a longer wavelength; the electron carries off the rest as kinetic energy.

Δλ = λC(1−cosθ),   λC=h/mec = 2.426 pm

What you're looking at

A momentum vector diagram for a single collision. The grey arrow is the incoming photon's momentum p₀ = h/λ₀. The red arrow is the scattered photon leaving at angle θ. The teal arrow is the recoiling electron, drawn as the vector that closes the triangle — because momentum conservation says exactly that: p₀ = p′ + pe, so the electron's momentum is the vector difference of the two photon arrows.

Watch the red arrow as you change θ. It is always shorter than the grey one, and more so at large angles. Since p = h/λ, a shorter momentum arrow means a longer wavelength: the photon leaves the collision redder than it arrived. The energy it lost is the energy the electron carried away, and the readout tracks both sides of that ledger.

Try this

Sweep θ from 0 to 180° at fixed λ₀. At θ = 0 nothing happens at all — a photon that misses is a photon unchanged. At θ = 90° the shift is exactly one Compton wavelength, 2.426 pm. At θ = 180°, a straight backscatter, it is the maximum 4.85 pm and the electron is knocked squarely forward. Now the important experiment: hold θ at 180° and change λ₀ instead. At λ₀ = 2 pm the wavelength more than triples and the electron makes off with over two-thirds of the photon's energy. At λ₀ = 100 pm the identical 4.85 pm shift is a mere 5% change and the electron gets a few hundred eV.

The absolute shift is the whole point

That last comparison is the key to the effect. Δλ = λC(1 − cosθ) contains no λ₀ anywhere — the shift is a fixed number of picometres set only by the scattering angle and by λC = h/mec = 2.426 pm, a constant built from the electron's mass. Whether that fixed shift is dramatic or invisible depends entirely on what you compare it against.

This is why Compton needed x-rays. Visible light at 550 nm is 550,000 pm; the same 4.85 pm shift would be a change of 0.0009%, far below anything detectable, which is why centuries of optics gave no hint of the effect. At x-ray wavelengths of a few picometres it is impossible to miss. The effect was always there in visible light — it was simply swamped.

Why this settled the argument

The photoelectric effect showed that light delivers energy in quanta, but a determined classical physicist could still argue about how the energy got concentrated. Compton scattering is harder to evade: the numbers come out right only if you treat the photon as a particle carrying momentum p = h/λ and apply ordinary conservation of energy and momentum to a two-body collision, exactly as you would for billiard balls. A photon has no rest mass, so E² = (mc²)² + (pc)² collapses to E = pc — momentum without mass, which has no classical counterpart at all. That the predicted angular dependence matches measurement to high precision is about as direct as evidence gets.

Bragg Diffraction Eq. 2.13

Rays reflected from successive crystal planes interfere constructively only at angles satisfying Bragg's law. Sweep θ and watch the path-difference counter lock onto integers.

2d sinθ = nλ,   n = 1, 2, 3, …

What you're looking at

Two parallel planes of atoms in a crystal, separated by d, with x-rays coming in at a glancing angle θ. The teal ray reflects from the upper plane and the red ray from the lower one. The red ray has further to travel — down into the crystal and back out — and that extra distance works out to exactly 2d sinθ. If it equals a whole number of wavelengths the two rays emerge in step and reinforce; if not, they arrive out of phase and largely cancel.

The plot underneath sweeps θ across the full range and shows where the reinforcement happens: a set of sharp spikes at the angles satisfying Bragg's law, with near-darkness between them. The vertical red marker shows where your current θ sits. Diffraction from a crystal is not a smooth gradual brightening — it is silence punctuated by narrow, precisely located flashes, and that sharpness is what makes the technique so powerful for measurement.

Try this

With d = 0.28 nm and λ = 0.15 nm, drag θ slowly upward. The first-order peak (n = 1) arrives at about 15.5°, second order at 32.4°, third at 53.5° — and then nothing, because fourth order would require sinθ > 1. Watch the "(2d sinθ)/λ" line in the readout and treat it as a counter: every time it passes through a whole number you are on a peak. Now try d = 0.10 nm with λ = 0.30 nm and sweep the entire range — there is no peak anywhere, because λ exceeds 2d and even n = 1 is unreachable.

Why crystals, and why x-rays

That last case is the constraint that governs the whole technique: since sinθ cannot exceed 1, diffraction requires λ ≤ 2d. To probe a structure you need a wavelength comparable to or smaller than the spacing you are trying to resolve. Atomic planes sit a few tenths of a nanometre apart, so visible light at 550 nm is too coarse by a factor of a couple of thousand and returns no information whatever. X-rays, at wavelengths of tenths of a nanometre and below, are the right size — which is why the discovery that crystals diffract x-rays simultaneously established that x-rays are waves and handed us a ruler for atomic spacing.

Run the logic in reverse and the technique becomes a measuring instrument. If you know d for a reference crystal, then measuring the angles of the peaks tells you λ for an unknown x-ray beam. This is precisely how wavelengths were determined in the Compton experiment — the picometre-scale shifts in the previous card were read off as shifts in Bragg angles, not measured directly.

The catch

The intensity curve here is schematic: the peak positions come straight from Bragg's law and are exact, but their relative heights and widths in a real crystal depend on how many planes contribute, on what atoms sit where within each cell, and on thermal motion. Extracting a full crystal structure from real diffraction data is the science of crystallography, and reading the peak positions is only its first step. Note also that the same geometry later applies to particles: when electrons were found to diffract from crystals according to this identical formula, it confirmed that matter has wave properties too — the subject that opens Chapter 3.

Pair Production Sec. 2.8, Example 2.5

The most literal demonstration of E₀ = mc² available: a photon vanishes and two particles of matter appear in its place. Energy does not merely convert into mass here — it becomes two objects that can be caught, tracked and weighed.

hν → e⁻ + e⁺    requires hν ≥ 2m₀c² = 1.022 MeV,   and a nearby nucleus

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the event: a photon arrives, passes close to an atomic nucleus, and converts into an electron and a positron. The two tracks curl away in opposite directions, as they would in a bubble chamber's magnetic field, since the particles carry equal and opposite charge. Raise the energy and the tracks straighten, because stiffer momentum bends less — in a real detector, that curvature is how the particles' momenta get measured.

The lower panel is the energy ledger. The red dashed floor is the 1.022 MeV that must be paid to create two electron masses, and it never changes. Everything above it, shaded teal, is kinetic energy shared between the pair. Below threshold there is no event at all: the photon cannot make half an electron, so it makes nothing and carries on.

Try this

Sweep the energy up from 0.2 MeV and watch nothing happen until you cross 1.022 MeV, where the pair abruptly appears with essentially zero kinetic energy. Just above threshold the particles are barely moving and almost the entire photon energy has gone into rest mass. At 2.00 MeV the split is roughly half and half. By 10 MeV only about 10% is spent on rest mass and the rest is motion, with each particle at γ ≈ 9.8 and moving at 0.995c. The threshold is a hard wall, but once past it the cost of the mass becomes a smaller and smaller share of the bill.

Why the nucleus has to be there

Beiser's Example 2.5 asks you to show that pair production cannot happen in empty space, and the argument is worth following because it is pure bookkeeping. Suppose a photon of energy hν turned into a pair flying off symmetrically at angle θ either side of the original direction. Conservation of energy demands hν = 2γm₀c². Conservation of momentum along the photon's direction demands hν/c = 2γm₀υcosθ, which is to say hν = 2γm₀c²(υ/c)cosθ.

Since υ/c is less than 1 and cosθ is at most 1, the second equation gives a strictly smaller value of hν than the first demands. The two conditions cannot both hold. A massive nucleus sitting nearby resolves the contradiction by absorbing the surplus momentum — and because it is so much heavier than the pair, it can take up momentum while carrying away almost no energy, leaving the energy ledger in the lower panel essentially untouched. The nucleus is a bystander that makes the books balance.

The catch

The process runs backwards too, and more easily. An electron meeting a positron annihilates into two photons of 0.511 MeV each — and that needs no nucleus, because two photons going opposite ways can conserve momentum between themselves. This is the basis of PET scanning, where a positron-emitting tracer is injected and the pair of back-to-back 0.511 MeV photons is detected to locate where the annihilation happened. Note also that pair production is the third of the three processes hiding inside the attenuation coefficient μ in the next card, and the only one with a threshold: below 1.022 MeV it contributes exactly nothing.

Exponential Attenuation of a Photon Beam Eq. 2.25, 2.26

Whatever the mix of photoelectric, Compton, and pair-production losses, the beam intensity falls off exponentially with a single coefficient μ.

I = I₀e−μx     x1/2 = ln2/μ = 0.693/μ

What you're looking at

The fraction of a photon beam that survives passage through an absorber of thickness x. The teal dashed lines mark the half-thickness, the depth that removes exactly half the beam. The defining feature of an exponential is that this distance is the same wherever you start: the next half-thickness removes half of what remains, and the one after that half again. The beam is never fully stopped, only reduced — which is why radiation shielding is quoted as an attenuation factor rather than a thickness that "blocks" the radiation.

Try this

Work Beiser's Example 2.7: 2.0-MeV gamma rays in water have μ = 4.9 m⁻¹, which is μ = 0.05 cm⁻¹ in these units. Set that and put x = 10 cm — about 61% of the beam gets through, and the half-thickness is around 14 cm. To cut the beam to 1% you would need about 92 cm of water — the book's 94 cm, using the unrounded μ = 0.049 — which you can sanity-check by noting that 1% is a little under seven halvings. Now push μ to 2 cm⁻¹, closer to a dense shielding material: the half-thickness collapses to about 3.5 mm and the curve falls off a cliff. That enormous range in μ is why shielding is made of lead rather than water when space is tight.

Why exponential, and why that is a quantum statement

The exponential form is not an empirical curve fit — it follows from a single assumption, that each photon has a constant probability per unit distance of being removed from the beam, independent of how far it has already travelled. Photons do not wear out or gradually weaken. Each one either passes through untouched or is removed in a single event, and the beam dims because it contains fewer photons, not weaker ones.

This is worth pausing on, because it is where the particle picture shows up in a place you might not expect. A classical wave pushing through an absorbing medium would lose amplitude smoothly and continuously, every part of the wavefront weakening together. What the exponential law actually describes is a population of individual quanta being picked off one at a time — the surviving photons at depth x are every bit as energetic as the ones that went in.

What μ is hiding

The single coefficient μ is a sum over three quite different removal mechanisms, each dominant in its own energy range: the photoelectric effect at low photon energies, Compton scattering in the middle, and pair production above 1.02 MeV, where a photon can convert into an electron–positron pair. That last threshold is set by the rest energy of the two particles created, 2mec² — and pair production cannot occur in empty space at all, since a nearby nucleus is needed to take up momentum while conserving energy. So μ is a property of the material and of the photon energy together, not a fixed constant of the substance; the value used in the example above holds specifically for 2.0-MeV photons in water.

Photons in a Gravitational Field Eq. 2.28, 2.29

A photon has no rest mass, yet gravity still acts on it. Drop one down a tower and it gains energy exactly as a stone would — but having nowhere to put that energy as speed, it puts it into frequency instead.

falling through H:   ν′ = ν(1 + gH/c²)   |   escaping a star:   Δν/ν = −GM/c²R

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the laboratory version: a photon falling down a tower of height H, arriving with a slightly higher frequency than it left with. The wavelength change drawn there is exaggerated by something like fourteen orders of magnitude, because at the real scale nothing whatever would be visible — the number printed underneath is the honest one.

The lower panel is the astronomical version, plotting the red shift of a photon escaping a body against how close that body's radius is to its own Schwarzschild radius. The red dashed curve is Beiser's Eq. 2.29; the solid teal curve is the exact general-relativistic result. They agree wherever gravity is weak, which is everywhere on the right-hand side of the plot, and part company only as the object approaches its horizon.

The argument, which is disarmingly simple

Drop a stone of mass m through a height H and it gains energy mgH. A photon cannot speed up, since it already travels at c. But it has energy hν, and by E = mc² that energy behaves gravitationally like a mass of hν/c². Give it the same energy gain that a mass of hν/c² would receive, and hν′ = hν + (hν/c²)gH, which is Eq. 2.28. The photon banks its winnings as frequency.

Try this

Set H = 22.5 m, the height of the tower at Harvard that Pound and Rebka used in 1960. The fractional shift is 2.45 × 10⁻¹⁵ — about two parts in a thousand trillion. For the red light in Beiser's Example 2.8 that is a change of 1.8 Hz out of 7.3 × 10¹⁴. Detecting a shift that small is the real achievement, and it took the Mössbauer effect, which produces gamma rays with an extraordinarily sharp frequency, to do it. Then work down the object menu in the lower panel: Earth gives 7 × 10⁻¹⁰, the Sun 2 × 10⁻⁶, Sirius B 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ — which is why white dwarfs were where the effect was first seen astronomically — and a neutron star around 0.17, a shift of 17% that no one could miss.

The catch

Beiser is careful to flag that his own formula is an approximation. Taken literally it says a photon can never escape once GM/c²R reaches 1; general relativity, worked through properly, gives the criterion as 1/2, so the horizon sits at the Schwarzschild radius RS = 2GM/c². You can watch that discrepancy appear in the lower panel: the two curves lie on top of each other until R is within an order of magnitude or so of RS, then diverge sharply, with only the exact curve running away to infinity at the horizon itself. This is a good habit to notice — an argument built from special relativity plus Newtonian gravity gets the weak-field answer exactly right and the strong-field answer wrong by a factor of two. Note finally that this red shift has nothing to do with the Doppler shift in the Chapter 1 card: nothing here is receding. The photon is losing energy climbing out of a gravitational well, not being stretched by relative motion.

Chapter Three

Wave Properties of Particles

Chapter 2 forced light to behave like a particle. This chapter runs the argument the other way and finds that matter behaves like a wave — which turns out to be where energy quantization, the structure of the atom, and the limits of measurement all come from.

De Broglie Wavelength Eq. 3.2

De Broglie's proposal was to take the photon relation λ = h/p and apply it to everything, matter included. The formula is trivial; the consequences are not.

λ = h / γmυ   —   the same relation that holds for a photon, now applied to matter

What you're looking at

A single axis covering forty powers of ten, from well below nuclear dimensions up to human scale. Two markers sit on it: the grey one is the size of the object itself (or of whatever structure it would have to interact with), and the red one is its de Broglie wavelength. The distance between them is the entire story of why the wave nature of matter went unnoticed for so long.

Wave behaviour becomes apparent only when a wavelength is comparable to the things it meets — that is the same condition that governs whether light diffracts through a slit or sails straight past an obstacle. So the question is never "is this object a wave?" but "is its wavelength big enough to matter here?"

Try this

Work Beiser's Example 3.1 in both halves. A golf ball at 30 m/s has a wavelength of 4.8 × 10⁻³⁴ m — twenty powers of ten smaller than a nucleus, which is why no golf ball has ever diffracted. Switch to an electron at 10⁷ m/s and the wavelength is 7.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m, essentially the radius of a hydrogen atom. That single coincidence is why the wave nature of the electron is not a curiosity but the organising principle of atomic structure. Then try the C₆₀ molecule, a ball of sixty carbon atoms that has genuinely been made to interfere in the laboratory, and watch its wavelength sit only a little below its own size.

The relativistic case

The γ in the denominator matters as soon as speeds get high, and it can be worked backwards. Beiser's Example 3.2 asks what kinetic energy a proton needs for a wavelength of 1.00 fm, roughly its own diameter. The answer is 617 MeV — a proton must be relativistic before its wave nature is on the scale of a proton. This is the logic behind every particle accelerator ever built: to resolve small structure you need short wavelength, short wavelength means large momentum, and large momentum means enormous energy. Accelerators are, in the end, very expensive microscopes.

The catch

Nothing here says an electron "is" a wave, any more than Chapter 2 said light "is" a particle. Beiser's own framing is careful: the wave and particle aspects of a moving body can never be observed at the same time, so asking which is the correct description is not a well-posed question. What the formula gives you is a wavelength to compare against the situation at hand, and that comparison tells you which set of properties will be conspicuous. Notice also that the speed slider is logarithmic — at these ranges nothing else would fit on a screen.

Phase and Group Velocity Eq. 3.3, 3.16

A single de Broglie wave stretches to infinity and localises nothing. A real particle must be a packet of them — and the packet moves at a different speed from the ripples inside it.

vp = ω/k = c²/υ   |   vg = dω/dk = υ   |   vp · vg = c²

What you're looking at

The upper panel is a wave packet: many waves of slightly different wavelength added together, so that they reinforce in one region and cancel elsewhere. The dashed teal outline is the envelope, and the teal dot rides it at the group velocity. The black dot rides a single crest at the phase velocity. Watch for a while and you will see the crests visibly sliding forward through the envelope, overtaking it and dying at the front — the packet as a whole lags behind the ripples that compose it.

The lower panel plots both velocities against the particle's speed. The group velocity is just the particle speed, a straight line. The phase velocity is c²/v, which blows up as the particle slows down and only ever comes down to c in the unreachable limit v → c.

Try this

Set β = 0.771, which is Beiser's Example 3.3: an electron with a de Broglie wavelength of 2.00 pm, a kinetic energy of 292 keV, a group velocity of 0.771c and a phase velocity of 1.30c. Now drag β downward and watch the two dots separate dramatically — at β = 0.2 the phase velocity is five times the speed of light while the packet crawls along at a fifth of it. Slow particles have wildly superluminal phase velocities, which sounds alarming until you notice what is actually moving.

Why a faster-than-light phase velocity breaks nothing

The product vpvg = c² means that whenever the group velocity is below c — always, for a massive particle — the phase velocity must be above it. That looks like a violation of relativity until you ask what a phase velocity actually transports. The answer is nothing. An infinite, uniform wave train carries no information: every crest is identical to every other, so nothing about the position of a crest can signal anything. Information lives in changes to the wave, and changes travel with the envelope.

The group velocity is what corresponds to the particle, and it comes out exactly equal to the particle's own speed — which is the real result of this section. De Broglie waves were not invented to have this property; it falls out of taking λ = h/p and E = hν together and asking how the packet moves. That the answer matches the particle's velocity is the first sign that the wave picture and the particle picture are describing the same thing.

The catch

This separation of phase and group velocity only happens because de Broglie waves are dispersive: their phase velocity depends on wavelength, so the components of a packet travel at different speeds. That is also why packets spread out over time, a point that comes back in the uncertainty modules below. Light in a vacuum is the opposite case — every wavelength travels at c, no dispersion, and phase and group velocity coincide. Matter waves and light waves are not the same kind of wave.

Davisson–Germer: Diffracting Electrons Sec. 3.5

Diffraction is a wave effect with no Newtonian analogue whatsoever. In 1927 electrons were caught doing it, by accident, and de Broglie's guess became physics.

λ = h/√(2m·eV)   must match   nλ = 2d sinθ    (θ = 65°, d = 0.091 nm for this nickel crystal)

What you're looking at

The upper panel is a polar plot in the style of Beiser's Fig. 3.7: the electron beam comes straight down the dashed grey line, and the distance from the centre to the red curve at any angle is how many electrons scatter in that direction. If electrons were simply bouncing off nickel atoms like tiny balls, this curve would be a featureless near-circle. Instead a distinct lobe appears at 50°, and it grows and fades as you change the voltage.

The lower panel shows why. The red curve is the electron's de Broglie wavelength as a function of accelerating voltage; the teal line is the one wavelength this particular crystal can diffract, namely 2d sin65°. The lobe is strongest where the two cross.

Try this

Set the voltage to 54 V — the case Beiser works through. The de Broglie wavelength comes out at 0.167 nm and the crystal's Bragg condition wants 0.165 nm; they agree to about one percent, and the lobe is at full strength. Now sweep the voltage from 30 up to 80 and watch the lobe swell and collapse, reproducing the sequence of panels in Fig. 3.7. Nothing about the geometry changed — only the electron's wavelength did. Then change the plane spacing and watch the matching voltage move, which is the same calculation run backwards: measure the voltage that gives the peak, and you have measured d.

Why this was decisive

The accident is worth remembering. Davisson and Germer were doing a routine scattering study, and classical physics was describing their results perfectly well, until air leaked into the apparatus and oxidised the nickel target. Baking the target to clean it annealed the many small crystals into a few large ones — and suddenly the scattering showed sharp maxima whose positions depended on electron energy. They had accidentally built a diffraction grating with the right spacing.

What makes the result decisive is that two completely independent numbers have to agree. One comes from the electron gun: λ = h/√(2m·eV), containing Planck's constant and nothing about crystals. The other comes from x-ray crystallography: d = 0.091 nm, containing nothing about electrons. The Bragg condition then predicts where the peak must be, with no free parameters left to adjust. It lands at 50°, where it is observed.

The catch

The shape and width of the lobe drawn here are schematic — real intensity profiles depend on how many atomic layers contribute, on the inner potential of the metal that slightly refracts the electrons on entry, and on thermal motion. What is exact is the position of the peak and the wavelengths involved, which is what the argument turns on. Note too that this is the same Bragg equation from the previous chapter, doing the same job; the only thing that has changed is that the waves being diffracted are electrons. G. P. Thomson demonstrated the same thing independently that year, using a different method — and won a Nobel Prize for showing the electron is a wave, three decades after his father won one for showing it is a particle.

Particle in a Box Eq. 3.17, 3.18

Trap a wave between two walls and only certain wavelengths will fit. Since wavelength fixes momentum, and momentum fixes energy, a trapped particle can only have certain energies. Quantization is a standing-wave condition and nothing more mysterious than that.

λn = 2L/n   →   En = n²h²/8mL²,   n = 1, 2, 3, …

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the box with its two infinitely hard walls. The red curve is the wave function ψn, which must go to zero at both walls because the wave simply stops there — exactly like a guitar string clamped at both ends. The shaded teal curve beneath is |ψn|², the probability of finding the particle at each position, and the black dots mark the nodes, the places it is never found. The lower panel is the resulting ladder of allowed energies.

Notice what the ladder does as n increases: the rungs get further apart, not closer. Because E ∝ n², the gap between consecutive levels grows steadily, so the spectrum never settles into something that could be mistaken for a continuum by getting denser at the top.

Try this

Start with an electron in a 0.10 nm box, roughly an atom's width — Beiser's Example 3.4. The ground state comes out at 38 eV, with the sequence continuing 152, 342, 608 eV. These are enormous gaps, comparable to the energies of chemistry, which is why energy quantization dominates atomic physics. Now switch to the 10 g marble and drag the box out to 10 cm, which is Example 3.5. The ground-state energy is about 5.5 × 10⁻⁶⁴ J and the corresponding speed is 3.3 × 10⁻³¹ m/s — indistinguishable from a stationary marble. Watch the "n for a speed of 1/3 m/s" readout: it lands around 10³⁰. The levels are there, but no measurement could ever resolve one from the next.

The three conclusions

Beiser draws out three results here, and they hold for any confined particle, including an electron bound to a nucleus where the "walls" are not sharp at all. First, a trapped particle cannot have arbitrary energy: confinement restricts the wave, and restricting the wave restricts the energy. Second, a trapped particle cannot have zero energy. Zero speed would mean infinite wavelength, and an infinite wavelength cannot be fitted between two walls, so there is always some irreducible motion. Third, because h is so small, quantization only becomes conspicuous when m and L are both small — which is precisely why Newtonian mechanics works so well for everything we can see.

That second point deserves emphasis, because it has no classical counterpart at all. A classical marble in a box can sit perfectly still. A quantum particle cannot, and the smaller the box the more violently it must move — a fact that turns up again in the next two modules as the uncertainty principle, and again in Chapter 4 as the reason atoms do not collapse.

The catch

This is a deliberately crude treatment: infinitely hard walls, no potential energy inside, no relativity. Chapter 5 redoes it properly by solving Schrödinger's equation, and the answers here survive that treatment intact — which is why it is worth doing the crude version first. What does not survive is the assumption of perfectly hard walls: with a finite wall the wave function leaks a little way into the barrier instead of stopping dead, which lowers the energies slightly and opens the door to tunnelling.

Building a Wave Packet Sec. 3.4, 3.7

Where the uncertainty principle actually comes from. It is not a statement about clumsy instruments — it is a property of waves, and it would apply to sound and water just as well.

a localised packet requires a spread of wave numbers:   Δx · Δk ≥ ½   →   Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2

What you're looking at

The upper panel adds together a number of waves whose wave numbers are spread around a central value; the faint grey curves are a few of the components and the red curve is their sum. The lower panel is the recipe: one vertical line for each component, showing which wave numbers went into the mixture. Since p = ℏk, that lower panel is also the momentum distribution.

Drag the spread to zero and the packet disappears — you are left with a single endless wave of one exact wavelength. That wave has a perfectly defined momentum and tells you absolutely nothing about position, since it is the same everywhere. Widen the spread and a packet forms, sharpening as the spread grows. You cannot have both.

Try this

Set the spread to 0% first and note that the packet width is unbounded while Δk is exactly zero. Now push the spread up gradually and watch the product Δx · Δk in the readout: the packet narrows in exactly the proportion that the momentum spread widens, and their product stalls around a half and refuses to go lower no matter what you do. Separately, raise the number of waves at fixed spread. The packet gets cleaner — the spurious repeats away from the centre fade out — but the central width does not shrink. Only widening Δk does that.

Why this is not about disturbing the particle

The usual first explanation of the uncertainty principle is that measuring a particle requires poking it, and poking it changes its momentum. Beiser gives that argument too, in Section 3.8, and it does yield the same inequality. But this panel shows something stronger: the limit is already there before any measurement is contemplated. It is a mathematical fact about waves that a short pulse requires a broad band of frequencies, known to radio engineers long before quantum mechanics and true of any wave whatsoever.

What quantum mechanics adds is the identification p = ℏk. Once momentum is wave number, the bandwidth theorem stops being a statement about signal processing and becomes a statement about what a particle can simultaneously possess. The uncertainty is not in our knowledge of a definite value — it is that a state with both properties sharply defined does not exist to be known.

The catch

The product here is measured in the display's own units and the packet width is read off where the envelope falls to 1/e, so the exact number depends on those conventions; what is real is that it has a floor and cannot be driven to zero. A genuine Gaussian packet achieves the minimum of exactly ℏ/2, and every other shape does worse. Beiser also notes that in practice Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2 is rarely attained — more often the product is around ℏ or even h, so the principle is usually used as an order-of-magnitude tool rather than a precise bound, which is exactly how the next module uses it.

Applying the Uncertainty Principle Examples 3.7, 3.8

A negative-sounding principle turned into a positive tool. Confine a particle and you can put a hard floor under its energy — which is enough to rule out whole theories of what a nucleus is made of.

Δp ≥ ℏ/2Δx   →   KEmin from E² = (mc²)² + (pc)²

What you're looking at

A logarithmic energy axis with some landmarks on it, and a red marker showing the minimum kinetic energy a particle must have if it is confined to the width you have chosen. Squeeze the confinement and the marker marches to the right, because a smaller box demands a larger momentum spread, and a larger momentum spread means the particle cannot be slow.

Try this

Set the width to 5 fm, a typical nuclear radius, with the electron selected. The minimum energy comes out near 20 MeV — Beiser quotes the estimate as "must exceed 20 MeV", using KE ≈ pc for a particle this relativistic. That is Beiser's Example 3.7, and its consequence is remarkable: electrons emitted by unstable nuclei are observed with at most a small fraction of that energy, so nuclei cannot contain electrons waiting to be emitted. The electron in beta decay must be created at the moment of decay. Now drag out to 0.053 nm, the Bohr radius — Example 3.8 — and the answer drops to a few eV, the right order for the 13.6 eV that actually binds hydrogen. The same inequality that forbids the first case comfortably permits the second.

What makes this a tool rather than a complaint

The uncertainty principle is usually introduced as a restriction, but here it does real work as an estimator. It takes a single length — how tightly something is confined — and returns an energy scale, with no detailed model of the forces involved. That is why it can settle the question of nuclear electrons without anyone knowing the nuclear force: whatever holds a nucleus together, it would need to bind an electron carrying more than 20 MeV, and nothing observed about nuclear energetics allows that.

The same reasoning explains why atoms have the size they do. An electron pulled closer to the nucleus lowers its potential energy but, by this principle, must raise its kinetic energy — and the kinetic term rises faster. The atom settles at the radius where the trade-off bottoms out. Matter is as big as it is because confinement costs energy.

The catch

These are order-of-magnitude estimates and should be read as such. Example 3.8 returns about 3.4 eV where the true hydrogen ground-state kinetic energy is 13.6 eV — a factor of four out, which is exactly the accuracy this method promises. It gets the scale right and the scale is what decides the argument. Note also that the calculation switches to the relativistic expression when pc becomes comparable with mc², which is what happens in the nuclear case; using the non-relativistic p²/2m there would overstate the energy by a wide margin.

Energy, Time, and the Width of a Spectral Line Eq. 3.26, Example 3.9

The second uncertainty relation. A state that does not last forever cannot have a perfectly definite energy — which means no spectral line is ever truly sharp, no matter how good the spectrometer.

ΔE · Δt ≥ ℏ/2   →   Δν = ΔE/h

What you're looking at

The emission line an atom actually produces, plotted as intensity against frequency offset from the nominal transition. It is not a spike — it has a width, marked in teal at half maximum, and that width is set entirely by how long the excited state survives before radiating. Shorten the lifetime and the line broadens; lengthen it and the line sharpens. This is the natural linewidth, and no improvement in apparatus can narrow it.

Try this

Set the lifetime to 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ s, the typical interval between an atom being excited and radiating, which is Beiser's Example 3.9. The energy is uncertain by about 5.3 × 10⁻²⁷ J, giving a frequency spread of roughly 8 × 10⁶ Hz. Against a transition at 5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz that is a fractional width of 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ — sharp by any ordinary standard, and yet irreducibly finite. Now drag the lifetime down toward 10⁻¹² s and watch the line bloat; long-lived metastable states, at the other end, give the extraordinarily sharp lines that make atomic clocks possible.

Where the relation comes from

The argument is almost embarrassingly simple. To measure a frequency you count waves, and if you only have a time Δt to watch, you cannot know the count to better than about one wave. So Δν ≈ 1/Δt, and multiplying through by h gives ΔE ≈ h/Δt. A more careful treatment based on the shape of wave groups tightens this to ℏ/2, but the essential content is already in the counting argument: a finite window on a wave is a finite window on its frequency.

This has a reach well beyond spectroscopy. It is why unstable particles have a width as well as a mass — a particle that lives 10⁻²³ s has a mass uncertain by tens of MeV, so experimentalists quote its width alongside its mass and read the lifetime straight off the peak. The shorter-lived the state, the fuzzier its energy.

The catch

The natural width is usually the smallest contributor to what a real spectrometer sees. Atoms in a gas are moving, so their lines are Doppler-shifted by varying amounts, and that thermal broadening typically swamps the natural width by orders of magnitude; collisions broaden lines further still. Beiser makes exactly this point at the end of Example 3.9. Getting down to the natural width is a hard experimental achievement — it takes cold, dilute, carefully prepared samples — and the fact that it is even a meaningful floor is the physics here.

Chapter Four

Atomic Structure

Two things classical physics cannot explain about atoms: that they are stable at all, and that each element emits its own fixed set of wavelengths. Bohr resolved both by importing quantization — and de Broglie later showed where that quantization came from.

Rutherford Scattering Sec. 4.1

Fire alpha particles at gold foil and most sail through. A few come straight back — which, as Rutherford put it, was as incredible as a 15-inch shell rebounding off tissue paper.

tan(θ/2) = D/2b,   D = Z₁Z₂e²/4πε₀KE   |   N(θ) ∝ 1/sin⁴(θ/2)

What you're looking at

The upper panel is a single alpha particle's path, integrated numerically from the Coulomb force rather than sketched — the curve you see is the actual hyperbola the equations produce. The teal segment is the impact parameter b, how far off-centre the particle would have passed had there been no force. The lower panel is the angular distribution: how many particles scatter through each angle, on a logarithmic scale because the answer spans seven decades.

Drag b and watch the sensitivity. At 200 fm the alpha barely notices the nucleus. Bring b down to a few tens of femtometres and it swings through a large angle. Take it to zero and it comes straight back the way it came.

Try this

Leave the energy at 5 MeV on gold and set b = 22.8 fm — that is exactly the impact parameter for a 90° deflection, and the readout confirms it. Now set b = 0 and read off the closest approach: about 45 fm, which is where the alpha runs out of kinetic energy climbing the Coulomb hill and turns around. That number is the crucial one, because it is still larger than the gold nucleus, so the alpha never touches it and pure Coulomb repulsion explains everything. Then switch to aluminium and watch the deflections shrink with Z.

Why this killed the plum pudding

Thomson's model spread the positive charge uniformly through the whole atom. An alpha crossing such an atom feels contributions from all sides that largely cancel, and the strongest field it ever meets is feeble — the predicted deflections are a degree at most, and a large-angle scatter would require an impossible run of small deflections all pointing the same way. Geiger and Marsden found that about one alpha in 8000 came back through more than 90°.

The only way to get a field strong enough is to pack the positive charge into something tiny. Rutherford worked out the distribution that a point charge would produce, the 1/sin⁴(θ/2) law in the lower panel, and it matched the counts across the full angular range. The atom's positive charge and essentially all of its mass sit in a nucleus some ten thousand times smaller than the atom itself — which is why the readout's atom-to-nucleus ratio is worth staring at. Matter is overwhelmingly empty space.

The catch

This whole analysis is classical: Newton's laws plus Coulomb's law, with no quantum mechanics anywhere. It works because the alpha never gets close enough for anything else to matter — and Beiser flags exactly this, noting that the agreement would break down if the alpha reached the nucleus, where the strong nuclear force takes over. Higher-energy alphas do eventually breach it, and the departure from the Rutherford formula is itself how nuclear radii were first measured. The irony is worth noting: the experiment that revealed the nucleus needed no quantum theory at all, while explaining what the electrons outside it are doing needed nothing else.

Why the Classical Atom Collapses Sec. 4.2

Rutherford's nucleus creates a worse problem than it solves. An electron in orbit is accelerating, an accelerating charge radiates, and a radiating electron must spiral in. Classical physics predicts that no atom survives a nanosecond.

Fc = mυ²/r = e²/4πε₀r²   |   E = −e²/8πε₀r   |   radiated power ∝ a²

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the death spiral: an electron starting in a stable circular orbit, losing energy to radiation, and falling into the nucleus. The lower panel is the frequency of the radiation it emits along the way, which climbs without limit as the orbit tightens.

Both panels are damning, and for different reasons. The first says atoms should not exist. The second says that even during the brief moment one did, it would emit a continuous smear of frequencies rising to infinity — never the sharp, fixed, repeatable lines that every element actually produces.

Try this

Set the starting radius to 53 pm, the measured size of a hydrogen atom, which is Beiser's Example 4.1: the orbital speed comes out at 2.2 × 10⁶ m/s and the total energy at −13.6 eV, both matching experiment exactly. Then read the collapse time. It is around ten picoseconds. Hydrogen has been stable for thirteen billion years.

The size of the contradiction

What makes this so sharp is that every step is orthodox. Setting the Coulomb attraction equal to the centripetal force is Newton plus Coulomb, and it gives the right radius and the right binding energy — the classical calculation is not merely close, it is numerically correct where it can be checked. The trouble comes from a third pillar, electromagnetism, which insists that accelerated charges radiate. That is not a fringe prediction either; it is how every radio antenna works.

So three well-tested theories, applied to the simplest atom, produce something that disagrees with the most basic observation about matter: that it is here. Beiser's conclusion is the one that runs through the whole book — the laws that govern the macroworld do not simply extend down to the atom.

The catch

The number of orbits completed before collapse is large — of order 10⁵ — so the spiral drawn here compresses the winding drastically; at true scale it would be a solid disc. What is honest in the picture is the shape of the decay and the timescale in the readout. Note also what Bohr's fix does and does not do. Postulating that certain orbits simply do not radiate stops the collapse, but it offers no reason why, and Bohr knew it. The reason arrives in the next card, where those orbits turn out to be exactly the ones a standing electron wave can occupy.

The Hydrogen Spectral Series Eq. 4.6–4.9

Before anyone knew why, Balmer found that hydrogen's visible wavelengths obeyed a simple formula containing nothing but small integers. That formula is the clue Bohr needed.

1/λ = R (1/nf² − 1/ni²),   R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹

What you're looking at

The upper panel lays out every hydrogen line on a logarithmic wavelength axis, one row per series, with the selected series picked out in colour and the chosen line in black. Each series crowds together toward its short-wavelength limit, the dashed tick, which is the line you would get from ni = ∞ — an electron falling in from complete freedom. The lower panel renders the Balmer series as the eye actually sees it: this is what a hydrogen discharge tube looks like through a prism.

Try this

Stay in the Balmer series (nf = 2) and step ni up from 3. You get 656.3 nm (Hα, the red line that gives hydrogen nebulae their colour), then 486.1 (Hβ, blue-green), 434.0, 410.2 — converging on the 364.6 nm limit, exactly the pattern Balmer wrote down in 1885. Now switch to Lyman (nf = 1) and notice that the entire series is in the ultraviolet, and to Paschen and beyond, which are entirely infrared. Only Balmer happens to land in the visible — which is the sole reason it was found first, and why hydrogen's spectrum looked like four lonely lines for decades rather than the infinite ladder it is.

What the integers were telling everyone

Balmer's formula was pure numerology when he wrote it: a fit, with no physical content, containing two integers and a constant. Its accuracy was uncanny and nobody could say why. Rydberg then generalised it to the form above, so that every hydrogen series is the same formula with a different nf, and still nobody could say why.

The shape of the formula is the giveaway. A wavelength that depends on the difference of two terms, each fixed by an integer, is exactly what you get if the atom has a ladder of allowed energies and emits the difference between two rungs. Bohr said later that as soon as he saw Balmer's formula the whole thing was immediately clear to him — and the next two cards are what he saw.

The catch

The constant R is not fundamental in the way h or c are; it is built from the electron's mass and charge, and it therefore shifts slightly for other systems — which is the subject of the reduced-mass card below. Note also that emission and absorption are the same set of wavelengths seen from opposite directions. Sunlight crosses cooler gas above the photosphere, which removes exactly the wavelengths it would emit, so the solar spectrum is crossed by dark lines at these positions. Reading those lines is how we know what stars are made of.

Bohr Orbits as Standing Electron Waves Sec. 4.4, Eq. 4.12

Bohr's allowed orbits were a postulate with no justification for a decade. Then de Broglie supplied one, and it is almost embarrassingly simple: an orbit is allowed when a whole number of electron wavelengths fits around it.

nλ = 2πrn   →   mυrn = nℏ   →   rn = n²a₀

What you're looking at

An electron wave wrapped around its own orbit, with the radial wiggle standing in for the wave's amplitude. Slide to a whole number and the wave joins smoothly onto itself: a standing wave that can persist indefinitely, drawn in teal. Slide anywhere in between and the two ends do not meet — the dashed black segment marks the mismatch — and on each successive circuit the wave interferes with itself until it dies away.

That is the entire quantization condition. There is no extra postulate, no new constant, nothing imported. An orbit is allowed if a wave can stand on it, exactly as a guitar string supports only those frequencies whose half-wavelengths fit its length.

Try this

Set the slider to exactly 1.00 and read off the ground state: radius 52.9 pm, energy −13.6 eV, one wavelength around the circumference. Now go to 2.00 and watch the radius jump by a factor of four, since rn = n²a₀. Then park somewhere awkward like 3.47 and look at the join: the wave arrives back out of step with itself, which is precisely why no such orbit exists. Sweeping slowly through 3.0 and watching the mismatch close and reopen is the clearest way to feel what "quantized" means.

What this buys, and what it does not

Setting nλ = 2πr with λ = h/mυ gives mυr = nℏ directly — Bohr's angular-momentum condition, which he had reached ten years earlier by an entirely different route and could not justify. Combined with the force balance from the collapse card, it fixes the radii as rn = n²a₀ and the energies as En = −13.6 eV / n², and those energies reproduce the Balmer formula exactly, with the Rydberg constant coming out in terms of m, e, h and ε₀. A numerological fit from 1885 becomes a derivation.

What it does not do is explain why a standing wave should fail to radiate, and it keeps a definite orbit for the electron, which the uncertainty principle of Chapter 3 has already ruled out. Beiser is careful on this point: the wire-loop analogy is an illuminating step along the path, not the last word. Chapter 5 replaces the orbit with a three-dimensional wave function, and the sharp circles here dissolve into probability clouds — but the energies survive unchanged.

The catch

The radial wiggle drawn here is a visual device, not the shape of anything. The real electron wave does not oscillate in and out from the orbit; what the picture is encoding is phase, and the thing that matters is only whether the phase comes back to where it started after one circuit. Note too how small the orbit speeds are — about 0.7% of c in the ground state — which is why relativity can be safely ignored throughout this chapter, though not in heavier atoms, where inner electrons move fast enough for relativistic corrections to change the chemistry.

Energy Levels, Transitions, and the Correspondence Principle Eq. 4.15, Sec. 4.6

Every hydrogen line is the gap between two rungs of one ladder. And at the top of that ladder, where the rungs crowd together, quantum physics quietly turns back into classical physics.

En = −13.6 eV / n²   |   hν = Ei − Ef

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the energy ladder, drawn on a true linear energy scale so you can see the levels bunching toward zero rather than spreading out. The teal arrow is the transition you have selected; pointing down it emits a photon, pointing up it absorbs one. The lower panel tests the correspondence principle by comparing, at each n, the frequency of the photon emitted in an n → n−1 jump against the frequency at which the electron is classically orbiting.

The levels being negative is not a formality. It says the electron is bound — it would need energy supplied to escape — and the amount needed from the ground state, 13.6 eV, is hydrogen's ionization energy.

Try this

Set ni = 3, nf = 2 for Hα at 656 nm, the red line from the previous card. Then 2 → 1 gives 121.6 nm, deep ultraviolet — and Beiser notes that a hydrogen atom in the ground state will absorb exactly this wavelength to climb back up, which is where absorption spectra come from. Now run the arrow the other way, 1 → 3, and read 12.1 eV: that is Example 4.2, the energy an electron must deliver in a collision to kick a ground-state atom up to n = 3. Notice how little energy the high transitions involve — 7 → 8 is worth only 0.065 eV — because the ladder is crowding toward its top.

Where quantum physics rejoins classical physics

Classically, an orbiting electron radiates at the frequency at which it orbits. Quantum-mechanically it radiates at whatever frequency corresponds to the energy gap it jumps. These are completely different statements, and at small n they give completely different answers — which they must, since the classical account is the one that predicts collapse.

But follow the red curve rightward. As n grows, the ratio climbs to one: the photon from an n → n−1 jump has very nearly the frequency of the classical orbit. This is Bohr's correspondence principle, and he used it as a design constraint — the quantization condition had to be the one that made quantum and classical predictions merge at large quantum numbers. Beiser's Example 4.3 makes the scale vivid: a Rydberg atom with an orbit radius of 0.0100 mm, big enough to see with a decent microscope, has n = 435. At that n the two descriptions agree to a fraction of a percent, and such atoms genuinely do behave almost classically.

The catch

This diagram gets hydrogen's energies right and stops there. It says nothing about which transitions actually occur and how brightly — real spectra obey selection rules, and some transitions between these levels are forbidden or extremely slow. It also shows one level per n, whereas each n conceals a set of substates that split apart in a magnetic field and differ slightly even without one. Those refinements need the quantum mechanics of Chapters 5 to 7. What is remarkable is how much this single formula already explains with one integer.

Reduced Mass and the Isotope Shift Eq. 4.22, 4.23

The nucleus does not sit still — it and the electron both orbit their common centre of mass. The correction is five parts in ten thousand, and it was enough to discover a new isotope.

m′ = mM/(m+M)   →   En = (m′/m) × (−13.6 eV Z²/n²)

What you're looking at

The same spectral line — the n → 2 transition — computed for six different two-body systems and laid out on a common wavelength ruler. The axis auto-scales to the spread, so what you are seeing is the differences between systems, magnified.

All six obey identical physics. The only thing that changes is the reduced mass m′ = mM/(m+M), the effective mass of the orbiting particle once you account for the nucleus recoiling about the shared centre of mass. Heavier nucleus, less recoil, reduced mass closer to the electron mass, and the energies closer to the textbook values.

Try this

Compare hydrogen and deuterium at ni = 3. Hydrogen's Hα sits at 656.3 nm and deuterium's at 656.1 — a shift of about 0.2 nm, three parts in ten thousand. That difference is how Harold Urey discovered deuterium in 1932: he found a faint companion line beside each hydrogen line, exactly where a doubled nuclear mass predicts. Then try positronium, where an electron orbits a positron of equal mass, so the reduced mass is exactly half the electron mass and every energy is halved. And muonic hydrogen, where a muon 207 times heavier than an electron orbits a proton, pulls the orbit down by a factor of nearly 200 and the energies up by the same — the atom becomes small enough that the size of the proton itself starts to matter.

Why such a small correction is worth making

Without it, the Rydberg constant comes out as 1.0973731 × 10⁷ m⁻¹; with it, 1.0967758 × 10⁷. That is a difference in the fourth significant figure, and by the 1920s spectroscopy was already far more precise than that. A theory that fails in the fourth figure when the measurement is good to the eighth is a theory with something missing.

What makes the reduced mass satisfying is that it is not a fudge. It is the exact classical result for any two-body system orbiting a common centre of mass — the same correction you would apply to a binary star — imported without modification. The Bohr model was right; it had simply been solved for the wrong idealisation, and fixing the idealisation fixed the fourth digit.

The catch

Reduced mass explains the isotope shift but not the fine structure: look closely enough at any of these lines and it splits into components separated by far less than the H–D shift, from electron spin and relativistic effects that this chapter has no machinery for. Note also that positronium is a genuine two-body system where the "nucleus" is as light as the orbiting particle, so calling m′ a correction understates things — and it annihilates within nanoseconds, which by the energy–time relation of Chapter 3 gives its lines a measurable natural width.

The Franck–Hertz Experiment Sec. 4.8

Bohr's energy levels were inferred from light. Franck and Hertz confirmed them without using light at all — by firing electrons at atoms and watching a current drop whenever the electrons had just enough energy to excite one.

the plate current falls at every V = k × Eexcitation / e,   k = 1, 2, 3, …

What you're looking at

Electrons are accelerated through a vapour and collected at a plate; the curve is the collected current against accelerating voltage. It climbs, then collapses, then climbs again — and the collapses are evenly spaced, marked by the dashed lines at multiples of the excitation energy.

The mechanism is a sorting of collisions into two kinds. Below the critical energy, an electron striking an atom can only bounce elastically, and since the atom is tens of thousands of times heavier it loses essentially no energy and sails on to the plate. The moment it carries just enough energy to lift the atom to its first excited state, an inelastic collision becomes possible; the electron surrenders that entire amount at once and no longer has enough left to reach the plate against the small retarding voltage. The current falls off a cliff.

Try this

Keep mercury and sweep the voltage. The dips land at 4.9 V, 9.8 V, 14.7 V — evenly spaced, because at twice the voltage an electron has enough for two inelastic collisions on the way, at three times enough for three. The spacing, not the position, is the measurement. Then check the readout's emitted line: 4.9 eV corresponds to 253 nm, and mercury's ultraviolet resonance line is observed at 253.6 nm. Franck and Hertz looked for exactly that line during bombardment and found it, closing the loop between a current measurement and a spectroscopic one. Switching to sodium packs the dips closer; neon spreads them out.

Why this settled the matter

Spectroscopy shows that atoms emit certain energies. It does not directly show that atoms can only accept certain energies, and a determined sceptic could have argued that the sharp lines came from something about the emission process rather than from a ladder of internal states.

Franck and Hertz closed that gap from the other side. An atom bombarded with electrons of steadily increasing energy ignores them completely — accepting nothing at all — until a precise threshold, then takes exactly that quantum and no less. The atom is not just fussy about what it emits; it is equally fussy about what it will take. Coming within months of Bohr's paper, this was independent confirmation that the energy levels were real physical states rather than a bookkeeping device for spectral lines.

The catch

The curve drawn here is schematic in its detailed shape — real Franck–Hertz traces have dips that vary in depth and get broader at higher voltages, since electrons arrive with a spread of energies and can excite higher states once the voltage allows. What is exact and what the experiment rests on is the spacing of the dips and their identification with the first excitation energy. A subtlety worth knowing: the absolute position of the first dip is shifted by the contact potential between the electrodes, so no one reads the excitation energy off a single dip — you read it off the interval between successive ones, which is immune to that offset.

Chapter Five

Quantum Mechanics

Bohr's model works for hydrogen and fails for almost everything else, because it grafts quantization onto classical orbits instead of deriving it. Quantum mechanics starts over: a wave equation whose solutions are probabilities, from which quantization falls out as a boundary condition.

The Wave Function and What It Means Sec. 5.1–5.5

ψ itself is not measurable and has no direct physical meaning. |ψ|² is a probability density — and once you accept that, everything else in this chapter is bookkeeping.

P(x₁ → x₂) = ∫|ψ|²dx,   ∫−∞∞|ψ|²dx = 1,   ⟨x⟩ = ∫x|ψ|²dx

What you're looking at

The red curve is ψ, the wave function. The teal curve beneath is |ψ|², and the shaded area under it between your two markers is the probability of finding the particle there. Every integral in the readout is computed numerically from the curve on screen, including the normalisation constant — nothing is assumed to be normalised in advance.

Notice that ψ goes negative for some choices while |ψ|² never does. That is the first clue that ψ is not the physical object. A negative probability would be meaningless, but a negative amplitude is essential, because amplitudes are what interfere — and interference is the whole reason quantum mechanics needs a wave in the first place.

Try this

Keep ψ = ax and the default window from 0.45 to 0.55 — this is Beiser's Example 5.2. Normalising gives a = √3, the probability of finding the particle in that tenth of the range is 7.5% rather than the 10% a uniform distribution would give, and ⟨x⟩ = 0.75: the particle sits well to the right, because ψ grows with x. Then switch to ψ = sin 2πx and put the window right at the centre. The probability collapses to nearly nothing, since there is a node at x = 0.5 — yet ⟨x⟩ is still exactly 0.5. An expectation value is an average, not a prediction; here it names a place the particle is never found.

What ψ has to obey

Not every function can be a wave function. It must be normalisable, so that the total probability is exactly one — which means |ψ|² has to fall off fast enough at infinity to have finite area. It must be single-valued, since the probability of being somewhere cannot take two values at once. And it and its first derivative must be continuous, which is what makes the boundary conditions in the following cards so restrictive: it is precisely these requirements, not any new postulate, that force the energy to be quantized.

The expectation value is defined as it is for a plain reason: it is the average position you would get by preparing this state many times and measuring x each time. Beiser is careful to note that this cannot be done for momentum in the same way, because the uncertainty principle forbids a function p(x) from existing at all. Momentum needs an operator, which is why Section 5.6 introduces them.

The catch

The Born interpretation — |ψ|² as a probability density — is an interpretation, not a derivation, and it was contested for years. What settled it in practice is that nothing else works: ψ cannot be a physical wave in space because it is generally complex, and for a system of N particles it lives in 3N dimensions rather than 3. Note also the wave functions here are real for simplicity; in general ψ is complex and the density is ψ*ψ, which is what makes the time-dependent phases in the superposition card at the end of this chapter matter.

Particle in a Box, Solved Properly Sec. 5.8, Eq. 5.46

Chapter 3 got the energies of a trapped particle from a standing-wave argument. Here the same answer comes out of Schrödinger's equation, together with something the earlier treatment could not give: where the particle actually is.

ψn = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L),   En = n²π²ℏ²/2mL²

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the box, with ψn in red and |ψn|² in teal, plus the dashed grey line showing what classical mechanics predicts: a particle bouncing between walls is equally likely to be found anywhere, so its density is flat. The shaded stripe is the narrow window from 0.45L to 0.55L that Beiser uses in Example 5.4. The lower panel tracks the probability of finding the particle in that window as a function of n, against the classical answer of 10%.

Try this

At n = 1 the particle is found in that central tenth of the box 19.8% of the time — twice what classical mechanics says, because the ground state piles up in the middle. At n = 2 it drops to 0.65%, roughly one-fifteenth of the classical answer, because there is a node at the centre and the particle is almost never there. Two adjacent states, differing by a factor of thirty in where the particle is likely to be found. Then walk n up the lower panel and watch the values oscillate about 10% with a shrinking swing — by n = 10 the quantum answer is closing on the classical one, which is the correspondence principle turning up again.

Where the quantization comes from now

This is the payoff of the chapter. In Chapter 3 the allowed energies came from requiring a whole number of de Broglie wavelengths to fit the box — a physical picture, but one you had to supply. Here nothing is supplied. You solve the differential equation, and demand only that ψ be continuous and vanish where the walls are infinitely high. Those boundary conditions have solutions only for particular values of E, and out drops En = n²π²ℏ²/2mL².

Quantization stops being a postulate and becomes what always happens when you impose boundary conditions on a wave equation. Organ pipes and drumheads have discrete modes for the same mathematical reason. What is peculiar to quantum mechanics is not the discreteness — it is that the wave in question is a probability amplitude.

The catch

⟨x⟩ = L/2 in every state, including those where the centre is a node. That is not a contradiction: the average of a symmetric distribution sits at the centre whether or not any probability is there. It is a standing reminder that an expectation value is a statistical summary, not a location. Note too that the infinitely hard wall is a fiction — it is what makes ψ vanish exactly at the boundary and lets the energies come out so cleanly. Soften the wall and the answers shift, which is precisely the next card.

The Finite Potential Well Sec. 5.9

Make the walls high but not infinite and something classically impossible happens: the wave function does not stop at the wall. It leaks into a region the particle does not have the energy to enter.

inside: ψ oscillates  |  outside: ψ ∝ e−κx,   κ = √(2m(U₀−E))/ℏ

What you're looking at

A well of finite depth, with every bound level drawn as a horizontal line and the selected one carrying its wave function. Penetration into forbidden territory

Inside the well, E > U and the wave function oscillates. Outside, E < U and the equation changes character: instead of a sinusoid it gives a real exponential, decaying with distance at a rate κ = √(2m(U₀−E))/ℏ. The depth to which the particle penetrates is roughly 1/κ, which the readout reports — and note what makes that depth large: a shallow well, or a level near the top of it.

There is no way to catch the particle out there. Localising it well enough to say it is in the forbidden region requires, by the uncertainty principle, a momentum spread large enough to supply the missing energy. The penetrating tail is real and has measurable consequences, but it never produces an observable violation of energy conservation.

The catch

This tail is the whole of the next card. If the forbidden region has a far side — if the wall is a barrier of finite thickness rather than an infinite expanse — then the exponentially decaying wave arrives at that far side with a small but nonzero amplitude, and the particle can appear on the other side. Tunnelling is not a separate phenomenon; it is this leakage, given somewhere to leak to. Note also that this is the simplest possible well shape, chosen because it can be solved; real wells have rounded bottoms and the levels shift, but the leakage and the finite count of bound states survive.

Try this

Set up Beiser's Example 5.6: E = 1.0 eV against a 10 eV barrier 0.50 nm wide. (Beiser quotes 1.1 × 10⁻⁷, having rounded k₂; the order of magnitude is what the argument needs.) Now raise the energy to 2.0 eV and the exact probability jumps by about a factor of four (Beiser's exponential estimate gives a factor of two — the two forms diverge here because k₂L is not large enough for the approximation to be tight). Then put the energy back and double the width to 1.0 nm instead: transmission collapses by about seven orders of magnitude. That asymmetry is the practical lesson — T depends far more sharply on how thick the barrier is than on how energetic the particle is.

Why this is not a loophole in energy conservation

The particle is never observed inside the barrier with negative kinetic energy. What the mathematics says is that the wave function has a nonzero amplitude on the far side, so a measurement there sometimes finds the particle — with its full original energy, undiminished. Nothing was borrowed and nothing needs repaying. The barrier does not lower the energy of the particles that cross; it only reduces how many of them do.

Tunnelling is also not exotic. Alpha decay is a tunnelling process, and its ferocious sensitivity to barrier width is why nuclear half-lives span twenty-four orders of magnitude for a decay-energy range of a factor of two. Fusion in the Sun proceeds by tunnelling, since at solar-core temperatures nuclei simply do not have the energy to surmount their mutual repulsion. And the scanning tunnelling microscope images individual atoms by exploiting exactly the sensitivity you just measured: since T varies exponentially with gap width, a change of 0.01 nm — a twentieth of an atom's diameter — produces a detectable change in current.

The catch

The exponential form is an approximation valid when k₂L is comfortably greater than one, which is why the readout shows both it and the exact expression; at thin barriers or energies close to the top they part company noticeably. Something stranger lurks just above the barrier, too: when E exceeds U, transmission is still not 1 — the wave partially reflects off a downward step, exactly as light reflects off a pane of glass it is perfectly able to pass through. Classically a particle with more than enough energy always crosses. Quantum-mechanically it sometimes bounces.

The Harmonic Oscillator Sec. 5.11

Any system disturbed slightly from equilibrium oscillates harmonically — molecules, atoms in a crystal, fields. It is the most reusable problem in physics, and quantum mechanics changes two things about it: the energies are evenly spaced, and the lowest one is not zero.

Zero-point energy

The lowest level is ½ℏω, not zero, and the teal line in the lower panel marks the energy a classical oscillator would happily sit at — perfectly still at the bottom of the well. Quantum mechanics forbids it, for the reason the particle in a box could not be at rest: being motionless at a definite point means definite position and definite momentum, which the uncertainty principle does not permit. The ground state is the best available compromise between the two.

This is not a technicality. Helium stays liquid down to absolute zero at ordinary pressure because zero-point motion is enough to keep it from crystallising. The evenly spaced ladder matters too: a molecule's vibrational spectrum consists of lines at multiples of one frequency, which is why infrared spectroscopy identifies chemical bonds so reliably, and why the spacing between rungs never changes no matter how high you climb.

The catch

A real bond is only harmonic near the bottom. Stretch it far enough and the potential flattens out toward dissociation rather than continuing upward as a parabola, so the true levels crowd together at the top instead of staying evenly spaced — and eventually stop, because the molecule comes apart. The evenly spaced ladder is an excellent approximation for the lowest few states and an increasingly bad one after that. Note too that these wave functions extend forever, decaying but never quite vanishing, so strictly there is some probability of finding an oscillator arbitrarily far from equilibrium.

Superposition: Why Stationary States Are Stationary Sec. 5.4

Schrödinger's equation is linear, so any sum of solutions is a solution. That innocuous fact is what makes an atom able to radiate at all.

Ψ = a₁ψ₁e−iE₁t/ℏ + a₂ψ₂e−iE₂t/ℏ  →  |Ψ|² carries a cross term beating at (E₂−E₁)/h

What you're looking at

Drag the mix slider to either end and the motion stops dead. A single energy eigenstate is called a stationary state for exactly this reason: its time dependence is a phase factor e−iEt/ℏ, and taking |Ψ|² multiplies it by its own conjugate, so the phase cancels completely and nothing observable changes. Put any weight on a second state and the two phases run at different rates; their difference survives into |Ψ|² as a cross term, and the density comes alive.

Try this

Set n₁ = 1, n₂ = 2 at a 50% mix — the strongest possible beat — and watch the charge cloud swing from one side of the box to the other. Now change n₂ to 3 and notice the oscillation gets faster, because the energy gap is larger: the frequency is exactly (E₂−E₁)/h. Then slide the mix to 0% or 100% and watch everything freeze, however high the energy of the state you land on. A highly excited atom, left strictly alone in one energy eigenstate, does nothing at all.

Why an atom radiates

This is the missing piece of Bohr's model. Bohr postulated that atoms jump between levels and emit the difference, but a stationary state has a motionless charge distribution, and a motionless charge distribution does not radiate — which was the original reason for assuming the orbits were stable.

An atom mid-transition is in a superposition of the two states, and that superposition has a charge distribution oscillating at precisely (E₂−E₁)/h. An oscillating charge radiates, at its oscillation frequency, and that frequency gives a photon of energy E₂−E₁. Bohr's frequency condition stops being a postulate and becomes a consequence: the atom radiates for the ordinary classical reason, and the only quantum ingredient is which frequencies are available. This is also why selection rules exist — if the cross term happens to integrate to zero, the charge distribution does not oscillate and the transition does not occur.

The catch

The animation runs at an arbitrary speed; for a real atom the beat frequency is around 10₁⁵ Hz. Note also that the mixture here is a genuine superposition, not a statistical mixture of atoms some of which are in one state and some in the other — those are different things, and only the superposition sloshes. That distinction is what the cross term encodes, and it is where interference, entanglement and most of the conceptual difficulty of quantum mechanics eventually live.

Chapter Six

Quantum Theory of the Hydrogen Atom

Solve Schrödinger's equation for an electron in a Coulomb field and three quantum numbers appear — one for each dimension of space. Bohr's energies come back unchanged, but the orbits are gone, replaced by probability clouds with shapes, and by rules about which transitions may occur.

Three Dimensions, Three Quantum Numbers Sec. 6.3, Eq. 6.17

Nobody postulates these. Separating the equation in spherical coordinates produces three ordinary differential equations, and each one turns out to have acceptable solutions only for particular integer values.

n = 1, 2, 3, …    ℓ = 0, 1, …, n−1    mℓ = 0, ±1, …, ±ℓ

What you're looking at

Every quantum state of hydrogen, up to the shell you select. Each row is a value of n, split into subshells by ℓ, and each dot is one allowed (n, ℓ, mℓ) combination laid out by its mℓ value. Count the dots in any row and you get n².

The nesting is the point. ℓ cannot reach n, and |mℓ| cannot exceed ℓ — so the three numbers are not independent knobs but a strict hierarchy, and each restriction comes from a different requirement on the wave function.

Where each one comes from

mℓ is the easiest to see. The azimuthal equation gives Φ ∝ eimℓφ, and since φ and φ + 2π label the same physical direction, the wave function must come back to itself after a full turn. That happens only if mℓ is an integer. It is the same closure argument as de Broglie's standing wave in Chapter 4, now falling out of the mathematics rather than being supplied.

ℓ comes from the polar equation, which has well-behaved solutions only when ℓ is an integer at least as large as |mℓ|. And n comes from the radial equation, which is solvable only when the energy takes one of the values En = −13.6 eV/n² — the same formula Bohr obtained, now derived rather than assumed — with the further condition that n exceed ℓ.

Try this

Set the slider to n = 3 and count: one s state, three p states, five d states, nine in total. Then note what the readout says about energy — every one of those nine has exactly the same energy, −1.51 eV, because E depends on n alone. That degeneracy is peculiar to the pure 1/r Coulomb potential; in any many-electron atom the s, p and d subshells separate in energy, which is what builds the periodic table in Chapter 7.

The catch

This picture is incomplete in a way that matters: the electron also has spin, which doubles every count here, so the n = 1 shell holds two electrons rather than one. Spin does not come out of Schrödinger's equation at all — it has to be added, and only emerges naturally from Dirac's relativistic treatment. Chapter 7 introduces it, and without it the periodic table cannot be explained.

Where the Electron Actually Is Sec. 6.7, Examples 6.2–6.3

The quantity to look at is not |R|² but r²|R|² — because there is more room at larger radii, and the electron's chance of being at a given distance has to account for the size of the shell it would be found in.

P(r) dr = r²|Rnℓ(r)|² dr   —   the probability of lying between r and r + dr

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the radial wave function Rnℓ itself, with its zero crossings marked — there are exactly n − ℓ − 1 of them. The lower panel is the radial probability density r²|R|², which is what you would actually measure, with the most probable radius, the average radius ⟨r⟩, and the radius Bohr's model would have predicted all marked.

The factor of r² changes the answer completely. For the ground state |R|² is largest at r = 0, right at the nucleus — yet P(r) vanishes there, because a sphere of zero radius has no surface to be found on. The competition between a wave function that falls off and a shell volume that grows is what puts the peak where it is.

Try this

Start at 1s. The most probable radius comes out at exactly 1 a₀ — the Bohr radius, recovered from a theory with no orbits in it. That is a genuinely striking result, and it is why the Bohr model works as well as it does. But look at ⟨r⟩: it is 1.5 a₀, not 1, because the distribution has a long tail outward. The most probable value and the average are different things, and Bohr's model had no way to distinguish them. Then check Example 6.3 in the readout: a 1s electron is about 1.47 times more likely to be found near a₀ than near a₀/2. Now walk out to 2s and 3s and watch extra humps appear, separated by radii where the electron is never found; switch to 2p and 3d and the humps vanish again, since ℓ = n − 1 states have no radial nodes at all — and it is exactly those states whose single peak sits at n²a₀, right where Bohr put it.

What replaced the orbit

Bohr's electron went round a circle of definite radius. This electron has a probability of being anywhere, with a distribution that happens to peak near Bohr's radius for the circular-orbit states and looks nothing like it otherwise. The 2s electron, for instance, spends time in two separate shells with a spherical surface between them where it is never located — and asking how it crosses that surface is a question the theory declines to answer, because it has no trajectory to describe.

Beiser's Example 6.2 makes a related point: ⟨1/r⟩ for a 1s electron is exactly 1/a₀, even though ⟨r⟩ is 1.5a₀. Averages of different functions of r are simply different numbers, and the one that matters depends on what you are computing — ⟨1/r⟩ is the one that gives the potential energy, which is why the energy comes out exactly as Bohr had it.

The catch

The number of radial nodes is n − ℓ − 1 and the number of angular nodes is ℓ, so the total is always n − 1 regardless of how it is split — a pattern the next card makes visible. Note also that these curves extend forever: the probability of finding a ground-state electron beyond 10 a₀ is tiny but not zero, so the atom has no sharp edge. Every atomic radius you will ever see quoted is a convention about where to stop counting.

Try this

Why these shapes matter

The energy of a hydrogen orbital depends only on n, so shape costs nothing here. In every other atom it costs a great deal. An electron in a p orbital is distributed differently from one in an s orbital, so it experiences a different average shielding from the other electrons, and the subshells split apart in energy — which is what makes the periodic table have the shape it does.

Shape also determines how atoms bond. A directional p orbital can overlap strongly with a neighbour in one direction and not at all at right angles to it, which is where molecular geometry comes from: bond angles are angular nodes made visible at the scale of a molecule.

The catch

These are the mℓ eigenstates, which are complex functions and are cylindrically symmetric about z. The px and py orbitals of chemistry textbooks are real combinations of the m = +1 and m = −1 states here — equally valid solutions, since the two are degenerate, and more convenient when the interesting axis is set by a bond rather than by a magnetic field. Both descriptions are correct; which is useful depends on what breaks the symmetry. Note also that the picture is a slice, not a surface: the familiar lobed drawings in chemistry books are contours enclosing some chosen fraction of the probability, and the choice of fraction is arbitrary.

Angular Momentum Cannot Point Anywhere Sec. 6.5–6.6

Both the magnitude and the direction of the electron's angular momentum are quantized — and the two conditions are subtly incompatible, in a way that rules out the vector ever lining up with the field.

Why the vector cannot align

If L could point exactly along z then Lx and Ly would both be zero — all three components known simultaneously. The uncertainty principle forbids that for angular momentum just as it does for position and momentum: you may know the magnitude and one component, and knowing one component precisely leaves the other two entirely open. The gap between ℓℏ and √(ℓ(ℓ+1))ℏ is exactly the room those unknown components need.

The z direction is not special in itself. It becomes special only when something picks it out — a magnetic field, most usually, which is why mℓ is called the magnetic quantum number and why its effects are invisible until you apply a field. That is the next card.

The catch

For ℓ = 0 the magnitude is zero and the whole picture degenerates: an s electron has no orbital angular momentum at all, which is another thing Bohr's model got wrong, since it assigned the ground state one unit. Note also the language of "precessing around the cone" is a helpful image but not a literal description — nothing is going round. The cone simply expresses that Lx and Ly have no values until they are measured.

Selection Rules Sec. 6.9, Eq. 6.35

Energy conservation permits a transition between any two levels. Most of them never happen, because the integral that governs the transition rate vanishes.

Δℓ = ±1,   Δmℓ = 0 or ±1

What you're looking at

Teal arrows are the downward transitions the selection rule allows from the state you have chosen; the crossed-out dashed arrow is one it forbids, even though the energy difference is perfectly ordinary and a photon of exactly that energy would be a perfectly ordinary photon.

Try this

Start from 4d (n = 4, ℓ = 2). The electron may drop to 3p or 2p — both have Δℓ = −1 — or climb sideways to 4f, but it cannot go to 3d, 2s or 1s. The 1s ground state is two steps away in ℓ, so a 4d electron cannot reach the ground state in a single photon no matter how much energy the jump would release; it has to go through a p state. Now try starting from an s state and notice the only destinations are p states. This is what organises spectra into series of sharp, principal, diffuse and fundamental lines — those old names are where the letters s, p, d and f come from.

Where the rule comes from

The rate of a radiative transition is governed by an integral of the form ∫ψ*final · r · ψinitial dV, which measures how strongly the oscillating charge distribution of the superposition — the thing from the last card of Chapter 5 — behaves like a dipole antenna. When the two wave functions have angular parts whose product with r integrates to zero over all angles, the atom has no oscillating dipole moment at that frequency and simply does not radiate.

The physical content is angular-momentum conservation. A photon carries one unit of angular momentum, so an atom emitting one must change its own by exactly one unit to balance the books. Δℓ = ±1 is not a mysterious quantum restriction; it is a conservation law wearing different clothes.

The catch

"Forbidden" means forbidden for single-photon electric dipole radiation, which is by far the fastest channel — not impossible. Atoms in states with no allowed downward transition are metastable and can survive for seconds rather than the usual nanoseconds, eventually decaying by much weaker mechanisms such as two-photon emission or magnetic dipole radiation. Those slow channels matter enormously in practice: they are how lasers maintain population inversions, and forbidden lines from metastable states in thin nebular gas are prominent in astronomical spectra precisely because nothing collides with the atoms long enough to knock them out of it.

An orbiting electron is a current loop, so it has a magnetic moment. Put the atom in a field and the orientations that were degenerate acquire different energies — making mℓ visible for the first time.

Why this was such a strong test

The splitting is μBB with μB = eℏ/2m — built entirely from constants already known from other experiments, with nothing adjustable. Measuring the splitting therefore tests the theory quantitatively rather than qualitatively, and it confirms something no classical model predicts at all: that an atom's angular momentum has a discrete set of allowed orientations relative to a field. Before the field is applied those orientations are indistinguishable, which is why mℓ seems like idle bookkeeping until you switch on a magnet.

The catch

This is the normal Zeeman effect, and it is the exception rather than the rule. Most lines split into more components than three, with spacings that are not simple multiples of μBB — the anomalous Zeeman effect, which was a serious embarrassment for years. The resolution is electron spin, which carries its own magnetic moment with an anomalous factor of very nearly 2, so the total moment depends on how spin and orbital angular momentum combine. The normal effect appears only in the special cases where the spins happen to cancel. Chapter 7 takes this up, and it is the route by which spin was discovered.

Chapter Seven

Many-Electron Atoms

Hydrogen has one electron and the equation can be solved exactly. Add a second and it cannot — yet the periodic table, the whole of chemistry, follows from two additions to the theory: the electron has spin, and no two electrons may share a state.

Electron Spin Sec. 7.1, Example 7.1

Two orientations, not three, not five. An even number of orientations is impossible for orbital motion, which is the first sign that this is something genuinely new.

|S| = √(s(s+1))ℏ = (√3/2)ℏ,   Sz = msℏ = ±½ℏ,   s = ½ always

What you're looking at

On the left, the two orientations the spin vector is permitted, drawn like the orbital angular momentum of the previous chapter — same geometry, with |S| = 0.866ℏ but Sz only ever ±0.5ℏ, so the vector again cannot lie along the axis. That two is the crucial number. Orbital angular momentum always gives 2ℓ + 1 orientations, which is odd for every integer ℓ. Getting an even number requires a half-integer quantum number, and half-integers do not come out of the orbital equation at all.

Try this

The slider asks how fast the electron would have to spin if it really were a little rotating ball — Beiser's Example 7.1. At a radius of 5 × 10⁻¹⁷ m the equator would need to move at about 5 × 10¹² m/s, roughly seventeen thousand times the speed of light. Drag the radius up and down: you cannot rescue it. To get the equator below c the electron would have to be larger than about 10⁻¹³ m, which experiment has long since ruled out — scattering shows the electron behaves as a point down to far smaller distances.

What spin actually is

It is an intrinsic angular momentum with no internal motion behind it, in the same way that charge is an intrinsic property with no internal structure behind it. The name is a historical accident and the mental picture of a spinning ball is wrong, but the angular momentum is entirely real: it adds to orbital angular momentum, it is conserved, and it responds to magnetic fields.

The magnetic moment that goes with it is the strange part. A classical charge with angular momentum S would have a moment proportional to S with a factor of e/2m — but the electron's is twice that, a g-factor of very nearly 2. That doubling has no classical explanation at all. It comes out of Dirac's relativistic equation automatically, which is one of the reasons that equation was taken so seriously so quickly.

The catch

Spin was not proposed on theoretical grounds; it was forced by experiments the existing theory could not explain — the anomalous Zeeman patterns, alkali doublets, and the Stern–Gerlach result. Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit, who proposed it in 1925, were young enough not to be deterred by the faster-than-light problem, and Pauli had earlier dismissed exactly this idea for exactly that reason. The lesson is that the objection was sound and the conclusion was still right: the flaw was in the mechanical picture, not the angular momentum.

The Exclusion Principle as a Symmetry Sec. 7.2–7.3

Pauli's rule is usually stated as a prohibition. It is better understood as a consequence of something deeper: identical particles are genuinely indistinguishable, and that forces the wave function to be either symmetric or antisymmetric under swapping them.

ψ(1,2) = ±ψ(2,1)  —  fermions take the minus sign, and then ψ vanishes whenever the two states coincide

What you're looking at

Two particles in a box, each in one of the box's states, plotted as |ψ(x₁, x₂)|² over the square of both positions at once. Darkness shows how likely it is to find particle 1 at x₁ and particle 2 at x₂. The dashed diagonal is where the two coincide.

The left panel is the symmetric combination, which bosons use; the right is the antisymmetric one, which electrons must. Look along the diagonal: the antisymmetric map is white there, dead, whatever states you choose. Two electrons are never found at the same place, and no force between them is responsible — it is the shape of the wave function alone.

Try this

Set both particles to the same state, n₁ = n₂. The symmetric map is unaffected; the antisymmetric one vanishes completely, not just on the diagonal. That is the exclusion principle, and notice what form it takes: not a rule forbidding something, but the fact that the only wave function describing it is identically zero. A state whose wave function is zero everywhere is not a state that is hard to reach. It does not exist.

Now separate them again and compare the two panels away from the diagonal. Bosons bunch toward it and fermions avoid it, from symmetry alone. That tendency is called the exchange interaction, and although it is not a force, it has the consequences of one — it is a major part of why solids resist being compressed.

Why antisymmetric

If two particles are truly identical, swapping them cannot change any measurable quantity, so |ψ(1,2)|² must equal |ψ(2,1)|². That leaves ψ(1,2) = +ψ(2,1) or ψ(1,2) = −ψ(2,1), and nature uses both: particles with integer spin take the plus sign and are bosons, particles with half-integer spin take the minus sign and are fermions. Which particles get which is settled by the spin–statistics theorem in relativistic quantum field theory, well beyond this chapter, but the connection is exact and has no known exceptions.

The catch

The picture uses one spatial coordinate each and ignores spin, so "the same state" here means the same spatial state. Two electrons can share a spatial orbital provided their spins are opposite, because the total wave function, spin included, is then still antisymmetric. That is precisely why each orbital holds two electrons rather than one, and it is where the factor of 2 in the 2n² capacity of a shell comes from.

Building Up the Elements Sec. 7.4–7.6

Add electrons one at a time, each going into the lowest subshell with room left. That single rule, plus the exclusion principle, generates the periodic table.

subshell capacity 2(2ℓ+1)   |   filled in order of increasing n + ℓ, and for ties, increasing n

What you're looking at

Every subshell the atom uses, in the order they fill, with one dot per available slot and the occupied ones coloured by ℓ. Closed subshells are labelled as such. The configuration in the readout is generated by the filling rule, not looked up.

Try this

Walk from Ne (10) to Na (11) to Mg (12). Neon closes the 2p subshell completely. Sodium's eleventh electron has nowhere to go but 3s, where it sits alone, far outside a closed core — and its ionization energy collapses from neon's 21.6 eV to 5.1 eV. Everything chemistry says about sodium follows from that one loosely held electron. Then jump to K (19) and notice something that looks wrong: the electron goes into 4s rather than 3d, even though n = 3 is the lower shell. The n + ℓ rule predicts it — 4s has n + ℓ = 4 while 3d has 5 — and this is why the transition metals appear where they do. Finally try Cr (24) and Cu (29), flagged as exceptions: a half-filled or completely filled d subshell is stable enough to be worth borrowing an s electron for.

Why shells close where they do

A closed subshell is spherically symmetric and tightly bound, and it shields the nuclear charge from anything outside it very effectively. So the next electron added sees a nearly neutral core and is held weakly — which is the definition of an alkali metal. Conversely, an atom one electron short of closing a subshell binds an extra electron eagerly, which is a halogen.

The periodic table's rows are therefore just the capacities of the subshells in filling order: 2, then 8, then 8, then 18. The reason chemistry repeats is that the outer configuration repeats, and the reason it repeats is the exclusion principle forcing each subshell to fill and close.

The catch

The n + ℓ rule is an empirical regularity, not a theorem — it summarises the outcome of a complicated competition between nuclear attraction and electron–electron repulsion, and it fails for a handful of elements even below Z = 54. Those exceptions are marked here. More fundamentally, none of this comes from solving Schrödinger's equation for a many-electron atom, which cannot be done exactly for even two electrons; it comes from treating each electron as moving in an averaged field of the others. That approximation is the subject of much of modern computational chemistry.

What the shape proves

If electrons simply stacked up in a hydrogen-like way with no exclusion principle, every one of them would fall into the 1s state and the ionization energy would climb smoothly with Z, since the nuclear charge grows. There would be no periods, no repeating chemistry, and no periodic table. The peaks and troughs here are direct experimental evidence for shell structure, and by extension for the exclusion principle that forces it.

Mendeleev arranged the elements by their chemical behaviour half a century before any of this theory existed, and the arrangement he arrived at is this curve. That the periodicity he inferred from reactions turns out to be periodicity in a quantity as basic as the energy to remove an electron is a striking piece of independent confirmation.

The catch

This is the first ionization energy. Removing the second, third and fourth electrons gives its own pattern, with enormous jumps whenever you start breaking into a closed shell — which is often a clearer fingerprint of shell structure than the first ionization energy is. Note too that the transition metals between Ca and Zn are remarkably flat, because the 3d electrons being added lie inside the 4s electrons that get removed, so the outermost electron barely notices the growing nuclear charge. That flatness is why the transition metals resemble one another chemically far more than, say, C and N do.

Why only two values of j

Adding two quantized angular momenta does not give a continuum of possible totals — it gives a discrete set, and for spin one-half the set has just two members, ℓ + ½ and ℓ − ½. The vector diagram shows the two ways L and S can arrange themselves, and the magnitudes work out exactly right in each case. Nothing was created or destroyed; the states were merely sorted into two energies.

The catch

Spin–orbit coupling scales steeply with nuclear charge, roughly as Z⁴, because the inner field grows so fast. In hydrogen it is a 10⁻⁵ eV effect and takes careful spectroscopy to see; in sodium it splits the yellow D line into the famous pair at 589.0 and 589.6 nm, visible with a cheap spectroscope; in heavy elements it is large enough to reorganise the level structure entirely, which is why the simple scheme used here breaks down at the bottom of the periodic table. This also finally explains the anomalous Zeeman effect of the last chapter: the total moment depends on how spin and orbital angular momentum combine, and spin's g-factor of 2 makes that combination anything but simple. One bookkeeping note: Beiser's text gives the Hα fine structure as 0.14 nm, but his own Example 7.3 estimate leads to 0.016 nm and the accepted measured value is about 0.014 nm — so the 0.14 appears to be in ångströms rather than nanometres. Why Z minus one

The Kα photon comes from an electron dropping into a hole in the 1s shell. That shell normally holds two electrons, so with one knocked out there is exactly one left, and the infalling electron sees the nuclear charge Z reduced by the single remaining 1s electron's screening. The effective charge is Z − 1, and the Bohr formula with that charge gives the observed wavelengths. It is a rare case where a crude screening argument gets the answer essentially right.

The historical consequence was large. Before Moseley, the elements were ordered by atomic weight, which put a few pairs in the wrong sequence and left it unclear how many elements were missing. Moseley's law gave a direct measurement of Z — a count of the protons — and settled the order definitively. It also showed exactly how many gaps remained to be filled, and every one of those predicted elements was subsequently found.

The catch

The Z − 1 screening works well for Kα and much less well for the L and M series, where many more electrons lie inside and the screening constant is larger and less clean. Note also what makes these lines so useful analytically: they come from the deepest, most tightly bound electrons, which are barely affected by chemical bonding — so an element's Kα wavelength is essentially the same whether it sits in a metal, an oxide or a protein. That is why x-ray fluorescence identifies elements in a sample regardless of what compounds they are in. Moseley himself was killed at Gallipoli in 1915, at twenty-seven, two years after this work.

Chapter Eight

Molecules

Atoms bond because a shared electron spends time between two nuclei and pulls them together. Once bonded, a molecule can also rotate and vibrate — and those motions are quantized too, on energy scales four orders of magnitude apart, which is why molecular spectra look so different from atomic ones.

The Molecular Bond Sec. 8.1

Every stable molecule is a compromise between attraction that wins at long range and repulsion that wins at short range. The minimum between them is the bond.

U(R) has a minimum at R = Re, of depth De — the bond length and the bond energy

What you're looking at

The potential energy of two atoms as a function of how far apart their nuclei are. Far to the right it approaches zero — the atoms are separate and do not interact. Bring them together and the energy falls: that is the bond forming. Squeeze further and it rises steeply as the nuclei and their closed shells repel. The teal lines inside the well are the allowed vibrational levels.

The depth of the well is the bond energy, and its position is the bond length. Both are measurable, and both are what any theory of bonding has to predict.

Try this

Compare H₂ with N₂. Hydrogen's well is shallow and narrow — 4.5 eV deep at a bond length of 0.074 nm. Nitrogen's is nearly 10 eV, because it is a triple bond, and that depth is why N₂ is so unreactive that it makes up most of the air you are breathing. Then look at what the readout calls the energy actually needed to break the bond: it is less than the well depth, because the molecule can never sit at the bottom. The zero-point energy of the v = 0 level is a permanent tax, and it is why deuterium bonds are measurably stronger than hydrogen bonds despite identical chemistry — a heavier reduced mass means a lower zero-point level.

Where the attraction comes from

Two neutral atoms have no net charge, so the attraction is not simple electrostatics between the atoms as wholes. In a covalent bond it comes from an electron being shared: a shared electron can be near both nuclei at once, which lowers its potential energy, and being spread over a larger region also lowers its kinetic energy by the uncertainty principle. The next card makes this concrete.

The repulsion at short range has two sources, and the exclusion principle is the larger of them. Pushing the nuclei together forces their closed inner shells to overlap, and since those states are already full, the electrons must be promoted to higher energies. That cost rises extremely steeply, which is why the left-hand wall of the well is so nearly vertical and why atoms behave like hard spheres in most circumstances.

The catch

The curve drawn here is a Morse potential, chosen because it has the right shape and can be written down; the true curve is computed numerically and differs in detail, particularly high in the well. Note also that this is a one-dimensional slice through what is really a many-dimensional energy surface — a molecule with N atoms has 3N − 6 vibrational coordinates, and chemistry happens on the full surface, where saddle points between valleys are what we call reaction barriers.

Electron Sharing: the H₂⁺ Ion Sec. 8.2–8.3

The simplest molecule there is: two protons and one electron. It is bound by 2.65 eV, and the reason is visible in where the electron goes.

What you're looking at

The upper panel shows the electron's wave function along the line joining the two protons, and beneath it the probability density. The lower panel gives the total energy of the molecule as the protons are brought together, for both combinations.

The whole of chemical bonding is in the difference between those two panels' red curves. Add the two atomic orbitals and the wave function is large in the middle, so the electron spends real time between the protons, attracting both. Subtract them and there is a node exactly at the midpoint — the electron is excluded from the one place where it could hold the nuclei together.

Try this

Leave it on symmetric and drag R inward from 0.40 nm. Watch the charge build up between the nuclei and the energy curve fall to its minimum at 0.106 nm, which is the measured bond length of H₂⁺, and 2.65 eV below the separated atom. Then switch to antisymmetric: the density develops a node in the middle and the energy curve has no minimum anywhere — it just rises as the protons approach. Same two atoms, same electron, and one arrangement makes a molecule while the other cannot.

Why sharing lowers the energy

An electron confined to one atom is localised in a region about a Bohr radius across. Let it spread over two atoms and the region roughly doubles, which by the uncertainty principle reduces its momentum spread and therefore its kinetic energy. At the same time, sitting between two positive charges rather than beside one lowers its potential energy. Both effects push the same way, and together they beat the proton–proton repulsion over a range of separations. That range is the bond.

H₂⁺ is worth studying precisely because it has only one electron, so the calculation can be done essentially exactly. Adding the second electron to make H₂ roughly doubles the bond energy to 4.5 eV, and the exclusion principle permits it only because the two electrons take opposite spins — which is why the covalent bond is a pair of electrons, a fact chemistry takes as a starting point and quantum mechanics explains.

The catch

Writing ψ as a sum of two atomic orbitals is an approximation — the LCAO method — and it gets the qualitative picture right while underestimating the bond energy noticeably. The true wave function is contracted toward the region between the nuclei more than a simple sum allows. Note also the antibonding state is not merely absent: it exists, it lies above the separated-atom energy, and populating it breaks bonds. That is what happens when a molecule absorbs enough light to dissociate.

Rotational Energy Levels Sec. 8.6, Examples 8.1–8.2

A molecule is a dumbbell, and a rotating dumbbell has quantized angular momentum. The resulting spectrum is a comb of evenly spaced lines — and measuring the spacing measures the bond length.

EJ = J(J+1)ℏ²/2I,   I = μR²,   ΔJ = ±1

What you're looking at

The upper panel is the ladder of rotational levels, which spread apart as J rises because the energy goes as J(J+1). The gaps are marked: from J−1 to J the gap is 2JB, so successive gaps grow by a constant 2B. The lower panel is the absorption spectrum, where each line comes from one step up the ladder — and because the gaps grow by a constant amount, the lines come out evenly spaced.

The dashed gold line marks kT at room temperature, and it sits well above the first several levels. That is the key practical fact about rotation: at ordinary temperatures a gas is spread across many rotational states at once, which is why the spectrum shows a whole comb of lines rather than one.

Try this

Take CO at J = 1 — Beiser's Example 8.1. The moment of inertia comes out at 1.46 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m², the energy at 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ eV, and the molecule is turning at about 1.0 × 10¹² rad/s. (Beiser's Example 8.1 prints 3.23 × 10¹¹ for this last step, but both √(2E/I) and L/I give 1.03 × 10¹² from his own E and I — the printed figure is smaller by exactly √10, which is what you get if the moment of inertia slips one decimal place.) Then read the J = 0 → 1 line frequency: about 1.15 × 10¹¹ Hz, which is Example 8.2 — and running that backwards is exactly how CO's bond length of 0.113 nm was measured. Now switch to H₂. Its reduced mass is fourteen times smaller, so its lines are spread out enormously by comparison. Light molecules rotate fast and have coarse rotational spectra; heavy ones have fine ones.

Rotational spectroscopy as a ruler

The chain of reasoning is short and completely rigid. Measure the line spacing, get B. From B get the moment of inertia I. From I, knowing the atomic masses, get R. No adjustable parameters enter anywhere, and the precision is extraordinary — bond lengths from microwave spectroscopy are among the most accurately known quantities in chemistry.

It works in astronomy too. The CO line at 115 GHz is one of the most important in radio astronomy, because molecular hydrogen is nearly invisible while the trace CO mixed in with it radiates brightly. Almost everything known about where molecular clouds are, and how fast they are moving, comes from watching this one rotational transition.

The catch

A molecule must have a permanent electric dipole moment to absorb or emit this way, so H₂, N₂ and O₂ — symmetric, with no dipole — have no rotational spectrum at all, and the curves shown for them here are what they would have. That is not a technicality: it is why the atmosphere's main constituents are transparent in the infrared while trace asymmetric molecules like CO₂ and H₂O are not, and therefore why the greenhouse effect exists. Note also the molecule is treated as rigid; at high J, centrifugal stretching increases I slightly and the lines crowd together a little.

Vibrational Energy Levels Sec. 8.7, Example 8.3

Near the bottom of the well the bond behaves like a spring, so the levels are those of a harmonic oscillator — evenly spaced, with a zero-point energy that never goes away. Higher up, the spring gets soft.

Ev = (v + ½)hν₀,   ν₀ = (1/2π)√(k/μ)

What you're looking at

The same well as the first card, now with the harmonic approximation drawn over it as a dashed parabola, and the vibrational levels of each. The short grey ticks near the centre mark where the harmonic ladder would put each level — evenly spaced forever — while the teal lines are the real ones, which crowd together as they climb.

Near the bottom the two agree almost exactly, which is why the harmonic approximation is so useful. Higher up the real well flattens toward dissociation while the parabola keeps rising, and the levels converge to a limit rather than continuing indefinitely. Try this

Work Example 8.3 with CO: the absorption at 6.42 × 10¹³ Hz gives a force constant of about 1860 N/m — Beiser notes this is roughly 10 lb/in, a genuinely stiff spring — and a level spacing of 0.266 eV. Compare that with kT at room temperature, 0.026 eV: the readout shows the ratio is about ten, which means vibrations are essentially frozen out at room temperature and nearly every molecule sits in v = 0. Contrast that with the rotational card, where kT spanned several levels. Now step v up to 10 and watch the gaps shrink measurably below hν₀.

Why infrared spectroscopy identifies molecules

The vibrational frequency depends on the force constant and the reduced mass, and both are characteristic of the particular bond rather than of the molecule as a whole. So a C=O group vibrates at much the same frequency whether it sits in a ketone, an ester or a protein, and a spectrum becomes a list of the functional groups present. Beiser gives the numbers: an –OH group at 1.1 × 10¹⁴ Hz, C–C at 3.3 × 10¹³, C=C at 5.0 × 10¹³ — stiffer bonds vibrate faster, so the bond order can be read straight off the frequency.

The catch

The anharmonicity shown here matters in a way that goes beyond level spacing. In a strictly harmonic well the selection rule Δv = ±1 is exact and no overtones exist; real anharmonicity weakly permits Δv = ±2 and beyond, which is what produces the overtone bands that make near-infrared spectroscopy possible. Note also that this treats the molecule as a single oscillator; anything more complex than a diatomic has many modes at once, some of which are local to a functional group while others involve the whole molecule flexing.

The Vibration–Rotation Band Sec. 8.7

A molecule changing its vibrational state almost always changes its rotation at the same moment. The result is not one infrared line but two combs of them, with a conspicuous gap in the middle.

Δv = ±1 and ΔJ = ±1  —  so every line sits at hν₀ ± a rotational step

vibrating and tumbling — the two never happen alone

What you're looking at

The infrared absorption band, plotted relative to the vibrational frequency at its centre. Lines to the right are the R branch, where the molecule gains a unit of rotation along with its vibrational quantum; those to the left are the P branch, where it loses one. The dashed line at the centre marks where a pure vibrational transition would sit — and there is nothing there, because ΔJ = 0 is forbidden.

Try this

Take HCl and drag the temperature from 50 K up to 900 K. The band spreads dramatically as higher rotational levels become populated, and the two humps move outward — which means an infrared band shape is a thermometer. Astronomers and combustion engineers both use it that way, reading gas temperatures from the envelope of a band in a spectrum. Then compare the readout's ratio of hν₀ to 2B: for CO the vibrational quantum is some five hundred times the rotational spacing, which is exactly why the fine comb of rotational lines sits neatly around a single vibrational frequency rather than overlapping with the next one.

The missing centre line

The gap at the band centre is worth dwelling on. It is there because the photon carries one unit of angular momentum and must dispose of it somewhere — the molecule's rotation has to change by exactly one unit, up or down. A transition that changed the vibration while leaving the rotation alone would leave the books unbalanced, so it does not occur. The same conservation argument produced Δℓ = ±1 for atoms in Chapter 7; here it is producing a visible hole in the middle of a spectrum.

The catch

Real bands are not quite symmetric about the centre. The bond is slightly longer in the excited vibrational state, so B is slightly smaller there, and the R branch lines gradually crowd together while the P branch spreads out — at high enough J the R branch can even turn back on itself, forming a band head. That asymmetry is not a defect; fitting it gives the vibration–rotation coupling constant and, from that, how the bond length changes with vibrational state.

Three Energy Scales, Four Decades Apart Sec. 8.8

Electronic, vibrational and rotational energies are so different in size that each lives in its own part of the spectrum — and that separation is what makes molecular spectroscopy tractable at all.

Eelectronic ≫ Evibrational ≫ Erotational

What you're looking at

The three characteristic energies on one logarithmic axis, with the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum they fall into marked underneath, and kT at room temperature for comparison. Try this

Note where kT at 300 K lands: comfortably above the rotational quantum and well below the vibrational one. That single fact organises an enormous amount of everyday chemistry. Rotations are fully excited at room temperature and contribute to a gas's heat capacity; vibrations are frozen and do not — which is why the measured heat capacity of air corresponds to a molecule that can translate and rotate but not vibrate, a result that badly puzzled classical physics. Then switch between H₂ and O₂ and watch the rotational quantum shift by more than a decade, because it depends so strongly on the reduced mass.

Why the separation is useful

Because the scales are so far apart, a molecule's motions can be treated almost independently: the electrons rearrange so much faster than the nuclei move that the nuclei can be regarded as fixed while solving for the electrons, and the resulting electronic energy then serves as the potential well in which the nuclei vibrate. That is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, and essentially every molecular calculation rests on it — including the potential-energy curve in the first card of this chapter, which is exactly such an electronic energy plotted against nuclear separation.

It also means each kind of transition is studied with its own instrument and answers its own question. Microwave spectroscopy gives bond lengths, infrared gives bond strengths and identifies functional groups, and visible or ultraviolet absorption probes the electrons. A single electronic transition carries vibrational structure inside it, and each vibrational component carries rotational structure inside that — which is why a molecular band spectrum looks so much richer than an atomic line spectrum.

The catch

The separation of scales is excellent but not perfect, and where it fails the consequences are interesting rather than merely inconvenient. When two electronic states approach each other in energy, nuclear and electronic motion stop being separable, and the Born–Oppenheimer approximation breaks down — which is exactly what happens at the conical intersections that let a molecule convert absorbed light into heat in femtoseconds. That mechanism is how your DNA survives ultraviolet exposure, and how the retinal in your eye responds to a photon, so the cases where this chapter's central approximation fails are among the most consequential in biology.

Chapter Nine

Statistical Mechanics

A mole of gas has more molecules than you could track if you devoted the age of the universe to it. Statistical mechanics gives up on tracking them and asks only how they are distributed — and that turns out to be enough to derive Planck's law, the heat capacity of solids, and why metals conduct.

The Maxwell–Boltzmann Speed Distribution Sec. 9.3, Examples 9.3–9.4

Molecules in a gas do not share the energy equally. They have a characteristic spread of speeds, and the shape of that spread explains everything from evaporation to why the Moon has no atmosphere.

The catch

This distribution assumes the molecules are distinguishable and that any number can share a state — Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. For an ordinary gas at ordinary density that is an excellent approximation, because the states available vastly outnumber the molecules and the chance of two wanting the same one is negligible. It fails when the gas is cold and dense enough for the de Broglie wavelengths to overlap, which is where the quantum statistics of the third card take over.

The Energy Distribution and the Tail That Does the Work Sec. 9.3

Chemistry happens in the tail. The average molecule almost never has enough energy to react — what matters is the tiny fraction that does, and how violently that fraction changes with temperature.

What you're looking at

Try this

Set the activation energy to 0.5 eV at 300 K and read the fraction above it — a few parts in 10⁹. Now look at the last readout line, which recomputes that fraction 10 K warmer. Why the exponential rules everything

The fraction above a threshold goes essentially as e−Ea/kT, which is the Arrhenius factor that governs reaction rates, diffusion in solids, semiconductor conductivity, and the rate at which a protein unfolds. Each of those processes has a barrier, and in every case the number of attempts is roughly constant while the probability of success is exponentially sensitive to temperature. It is the single most useful formula in applied physical chemistry, and here it is simply the area under a tail.

The catch

The fraction above a threshold is not the same as a reaction rate — molecules must also collide in the right orientation, and the barrier itself may be surmounted by tunnelling rather than climbed, which matters greatly for hydrogen transfers at low temperature. What this curve gives is the dominant temperature dependence, not the absolute rate.

Identical particles cannot be labelled, and whether they are fermions or bosons changes how they distribute themselves. Both quantum answers collapse onto the classical one as soon as states are plentiful.

Fermi–Dirac never exceeds 1, because the exclusion principle forbids it. Bose–Einstein diverges as ε approaches μ, because bosons positively prefer states that are already occupied. Maxwell–Boltzmann sails past 1 without noticing, which is its tell-tale failure.

Try this

Look at the right-hand side of the plot, a few kT above μ. All three curves lie on top of each other — the readout shows BE and FD within about 2% of the classical value there. That is the crucial observation: quantum statistics only matter where occupancy approaches 1. When states are plentiful and occupancies are tiny, nobody is competing for anything and the bookkeeping does not matter. Now drag the temperature down and watch the three separate dramatically near ε = μ.

What each one is for

Bose–Einstein applies to photons, phonons and helium-4 atoms. Its divergence at low energy is not a defect but a physical effect: it is why a laser can put astronomically many photons into a single mode, and why helium becomes a superfluid. Fermi–Dirac applies to electrons, protons and neutrons, and its ceiling of one particle per state is what gives matter volume, holds up white dwarfs, and determines how metals conduct — which is the last card of this chapter.

The catch

The chemical potential μ is treated as an adjustable parameter here, but it is not free: it is fixed by requiring the total number of particles to come out right, so it shifts with temperature and density. For photons, whose number is not conserved, μ is zero — which is exactly why Planck's law has no μ in it, as the next card shows.

Where Planck's Law Comes From Sec. 9.5–9.6

Chapter 2 presented Planck's formula as a fit that worked. Here it is a consequence: count the modes, then ask Bose–Einstein statistics how much energy each one actually holds.

⟨ε⟩ = hν/(ehν/kT − 1)   instead of the classical   ⟨ε⟩ = kT

What you're looking at

The average energy held by a single cavity mode, plotted against how big that mode's quantum is compared with kT. Classical equipartition says every mode gets kT regardless — the flat dashed line — and since the number of modes grows as ν² without limit, the total energy diverges. That is the ultraviolet catastrophe, and you can see it here as a line that never comes down.

The Bose–Einstein result starts at kT for small quanta, agreeing with the classical answer exactly where the classical answer was tested, then falls away exponentially once hν exceeds kT. Multiply this curve by the ν² density of modes and you have Planck's law.

Try this

Set T = 5800 K, the Sun's surface. The marked point shows where 550 nm light sits: hν/kT is about 4.5, so such a mode holds well under a tenth of kT and contains on average only a fraction of a photon. Now drop the temperature to 300 K and watch that point run off the right of the plot entirely — at room temperature visible modes are essentially empty, which is why objects around you do not glow. The readout's occupancy number is the direct statement of that: the average number of photons in a 550 nm mode.

What Planck actually changed

Nothing in the mode counting. Rayleigh and Jeans had that part right, and it is a straightforward exercise in fitting standing waves into a box. What changed is the average energy per mode, and the reason it changes is that energy comes in lumps of hν. A mode whose quantum is large compared with kT cannot be excited a little; it must take a whole quantum or nothing, and the Boltzmann factor makes that unlikely. The cutoff is not imposed, it emerges.

Beiser makes the point that this is also derivable from Einstein's treatment of emission and absorption coefficients, which arrives at the same formula by an entirely different route and additionally predicts stimulated emission — the basis of the laser.

The catch

Photons are bosons with μ = 0, because they are not conserved: a cavity wall can create or destroy them freely, so the number adjusts until the free energy is minimised. That is why the Bose–Einstein expression here has no chemical potential in it, and it is also why a photon gas cannot Bose–condense the way a gas of atoms can — there is no fixed particle number to force into the ground state.

The Specific Heats of Solids Sec. 9.8

Classical physics predicts every solid has the same molar heat capacity at every temperature. It is right at room temperature and catastrophically wrong in the cold, and fixing it was one of the first triumphs of quantum theory.

Dulong–Petit: cV = 3R   |   Debye: cV → T³ as T → 0

What you're looking at

Molar heat capacity against temperature. The dashed grey line is the classical Dulong–Petit value of 3R, which every solid was supposed to have. The curves are Einstein's model, which treats every atom as an independent oscillator of one frequency, and Debye's, which treats the vibrations as sound waves in the solid as a whole.

Both go to zero at low temperature, which is the essential quantum result: when kT falls below the vibrational quantum, the modes cannot be excited at all and the solid simply stops absorbing heat into them. Einstein's version falls too fast; Debye's gives the T³ behaviour that is actually measured.

Try this

Start with copper at 300 K: the heat capacity is already within a few percent of 3R, which is why Dulong and Petit found their law working for most metals at room temperature. Now switch to diamond, whose Debye temperature is 2230 K — at room temperature it sits at a small fraction of 3R, and diamond was one of the conspicuous exceptions that made the classical law look broken. Beiser lists boron and beryllium alongside it for the same reason. Then take copper down to 20 K and watch the capacity collapse toward zero as T³.

Why the classical answer fails

Equipartition gives each atom kT of kinetic and kT of potential energy per dimension, hence 3R per mole, independent of everything. That assumes each oscillator can absorb an arbitrarily small amount of energy. When the available thermal energy kT drops below the vibrational quantum hν, the mode is frozen: it can take a whole quantum or nothing, and mostly takes nothing. Exactly the same argument that turned over the blackbody curve in the previous card turns over this one, with lattice vibrations in place of cavity modes.

The catch

Debye's model treats the vibrational spectrum as that of an elastic continuum cut off at a maximum frequency, which is an approximation to a real phonon spectrum that has structure in it. It works remarkably well anyway, and the T³ law at low temperature is exact for any solid. Note also that metals have a small additional electronic contribution, linear in T, which dominates at very low temperature where the T³ lattice term has died away — and why that contribution is so small is the subject of the final card.

Free Electrons in a Metal Sec. 9.9–9.10

A metal contains a mole of nearly free electrons. Classically each should carry 3kT/2 of thermal energy and add hugely to the heat capacity. They do not, and the exclusion principle is why.

f(ε) = 1/(e(ε−εF)/kT + 1),   εF = (h²/2m)(3n/8π)2/3

What you're looking at

The upper panel is how the electrons are distributed in energy — the density of available states, which grows as √ε, multiplied by the Fermi–Dirac occupancy. It is filled up to the Fermi energy and empty above, with a small blur at the top. The lower panel is the occupancy alone, with the perfect step it would have at absolute zero drawn dashed behind it and the smeared region of width a few kT shaded.

Try this

Take copper at 300 K. The Fermi energy comes out near 7 eV and kT is 0.026 eV, so the smearing is a fraction of a percent of the way down the distribution. The readout puts a number on it: only about 0.4% of the electrons are close enough to the top to absorb thermal energy at all. Every other electron is locked in place, because the states just above it are already occupied and the exclusion principle forbids it from moving there. That is why the electronic contribution to a metal's heat capacity is roughly a hundred times smaller than classical physics predicts.

Now look at the Fermi temperature — some 80,000 K for copper. Melting the metal does not come close to it. In thermodynamic terms the electron gas in a metal at room temperature is not hot at all; it is a degenerate gas, essentially at absolute zero, and heating it barely disturbs it. Then check the Fermi speed: over 10⁶ m/s, and that is the speed of the fastest electrons at T = 0, with no thermal energy involved whatsoever.

Why this matters beyond metals

The same physics operates wherever fermions are packed densely. The electrons in a white dwarf are degenerate, and the pressure of that degenerate gas — a pressure that exists at zero temperature, purely from the exclusion principle — is what holds the star up against gravity. When the star is too massive for even that, electrons and protons combine into neutrons and degenerate neutrons hold up a neutron star instead. Beiser closes this chapter with exactly that application, and it is a remarkable reach: a rule about two electrons in an atom, scaled up to the fate of stars.

The catch

The free-electron model ignores the lattice entirely, treating the metal as a box with a uniform potential inside. That it predicts Fermi energies of the right size is already impressive, but it cannot explain why some materials are insulators — for that the periodic potential of the lattice must be put back in, opening gaps in the energy spectrum. That is the subject of the next chapter.

Chapter Ten

The Solid State

Everything so far has been about one atom, or two. Put 1023 of them in a regular array and the sharp energy levels smear into bands, the gaps between those bands decide whether the material is a wire or a window, and a lattice that can be made to vibrate just right will carry a current forever. This is where quantum mechanics stops being a curiosity about spectra and starts being the semiconductor industry.

What Holds an Ionic Crystal Together Sec. 10.2, Example 10.1

Two competing terms, one attractive and long-ranged, one repulsive and brutally short-ranged. Their sum has a minimum, and the depth of that minimum is the energy you must pay to take the crystal apart.

U = −αe²/4πε0r + B/rn  →  U0 = −(αe²/4πε0r0)(1 − 1/n)

What you're looking at

Three curves. The dashed teal one is the Coulomb energy of one ion in the field of every other ion in the crystal — not just its neighbour. Summing that over an infinite alternating lattice is not obvious (the terms alternate in sign and the series converges only conditionally), and the answer is a pure number depending only on the geometry: the Madelung constant α. It is 1.748 for the rock-salt arrangement and 1.763 for the CsCl arrangement. Notice how close those are — the lattice geometry barely matters compared with how close the ions get.

The dashed gold curve is the repulsion that appears when the closed electron shells of two ions start to overlap. It is not electrostatic; it is the exclusion principle, which forbids the overlapping electrons from occupying the same states and so forces some of them into higher ones. Modelled as B/rn with n ≈ 9, it is almost a hard wall: halving r multiplies it by 512.

The solid red curve is their sum, and the marked point is the equilibrium. B is not a free parameter — it is fixed by requiring the minimum to fall exactly at the observed r0, which is why the curve always bottoms out on the dashed vertical line.

Try this

Leave it at NaCl with n = 9, which is Beiser's Example 10.1. The readout gives U0 = −7.96 eV per ion pair, so 3.98 eV per ion. But that is the energy to pull the crystal apart into ions, and cohesive energy is conventionally measured against free atoms — so you must hand back the 5.14 eV it took to ionize sodium, minus the 3.61 eV you got for attaching that electron to chlorine. That is 1.53 eV per pair, 0.77 eV per atom, and the cohesive energy comes out at 3.21 eV against a measured 3.28. A two-term model with one adjustable exponent, right to 2 percent.

Now sweep n from 5 to 14 and watch how little happens. Going from n = 9 to n = 10 changes U0 by 1.25 percent, because n enters only through the factor (1 − 1/n). This is the useful kind of insensitivity: we do not know the repulsion law well, and it turns out we do not have to.

Then compare LiF with RbI. Same structure, same Madelung constant, same exponent — and LiF binds nearly twice as hard, purely because its ions are small enough to sit 0.201 nm apart instead of 0.366 nm. Ionic bond strength is very nearly a story about ionic radii and nothing else.

Why the numbers come out where they do

The scale is set by e²/4πε0 = 1.44 eV·nm. Divide that by a typical interionic distance of 0.3 nm and you get about 5 eV; multiply by the Madelung constant and take off the 11 percent the repulsion costs, and you land near 8 eV per ion pair. Every ionic crystal in the table lives within a factor of two of that estimate, and the reason is simply that all ions are about the same size.

The catch

The agreement with experiment is genuinely good, but it leans on r0 being taken from measurement rather than predicted. A real first-principles calculation would have to derive the equilibrium spacing too, which means treating the electron overlap properly instead of parameterising it as B/rn. Note also the sign convention in the electron-transfer term: for CsCl the ionization energy of caesium (3.89 eV) barely exceeds the electron affinity of chlorine (3.61 eV), so almost nothing is spent creating the ions — which is exactly why caesium halides are such good ionic crystals despite their large spacing.

The Weakest Bond, and Why Argon Is a Solid At All Sec. 10.4

A neutral atom with no dipole moment still attracts another one, because "no dipole moment" means no average dipole moment. The fluctuations do the binding — and they bind a hundred times more weakly than anything else in this chapter.

U(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)12 − (σ/r)6]    Ucrystal/N = 2ε[A12(σ/R)12 − A6(σ/R)6]

Where the 1/r⁶ comes from

At any instant the electron cloud of an atom is not centred on the nucleus, so the atom has a fluctuating dipole moment p. That dipole produces a field that falls off as 1/r³, which induces a dipole p' ∝ 1/r³ in a neighbour, and the interaction energy of a dipole with a field is −p'·E ∝ 1/r⁶. Crucially the induced dipole is always oriented so that the force is attractive, which is why the fluctuations do not average to zero even though the dipole itself does. The repulsion is again the exclusion principle; the 12th power is a computational convenience (it is the 6th power squared) rather than physics.

What you're looking at

Grey is the potential between two isolated atoms. Red is the energy per atom in the face-centred cubic crystal, where the 1/r⁶ and 1/r¹² terms have been summed over every atom in the lattice — the sums converge to A6 = 14.454 and A12 = 12.132 in units of the nearest-neighbour contribution. Because those sums are pure geometry, the entire crystal is determined by the two numbers ε and σ measured on a dilute gas.

The bar chart below puts the result in context against the other four kinds of bond, with the measured melting points overlaid. The correlation is not subtle: melting is what happens when kT becomes a respectable fraction of the bond energy, so a bond ten times weaker melts at roughly a tenth the temperature.

With argon selected, read off the predicted nearest-neighbour spacing. The lattice sum gives R₀ = (2A12/A6)1/6σ = 1.09σ, about 0.371 nm against a measured 0.376 — under 2 percent, from gas-phase parameters alone. The cohesive energy comes out as 8.61ε per atom, roughly 10 percent above the measured value.

Then note the ratio 8.61: each atom is bound by more than eight times the depth of a single pair bond, even though it has only 12 nearest neighbours. The second and third shells of neighbours contribute the extra 20 percent, and no further — 1/r⁶ converges fast.

Compare the readout line "kT at the melting point" across all four noble gases. It is close to the same fraction of the bond energy every time, which is why neon melts at 25 K and xenon at 161 K in almost exact proportion to their ε.

Why it matters more than it looks

Van der Waals forces are the weakest thing here and also the most universal — they act between every pair of atoms, charged or not, bonded or not. They hold the layers of graphite together (which is why a pencil works), hold molecular crystals and polymers together, let a gecko walk up glass, and are the reason any gas can be liquefied at all. The 10 percent error above is a better result than it appears, given that the entire attraction is a second-order perturbation effect.

The catch

The model quietly assumes the interaction is a sum of independent pair terms. It is not: three atoms in a row interact in a way that is not the sum of the three pairs (the Axilrod–Teller correction), which accounts for most of the remaining discrepancy. It also ignores zero-point motion, which is why the error grows steadily as the atoms get lighter: xenon comes out 1 percent high, krypton 4, argon 12, and neon 33. Light atoms in a shallow well never sit still, and the energy of that unavoidable jiggling has to be subtracted from the binding. Helium is the limiting case — its zero-point energy exceeds its well depth, so it does not solidify at all under its own vapour pressure.

Free Electrons, Drift, and Where Ohm's Law Comes From Sec. 10.5, Examples 10.2–10.3

The electrons in a copper wire move at a million metres per second. The current moves at a fraction of a millimetre per second. Both are true, and the gap between them is the whole story.

υd = I/nAe    ρ = mυF/ne²λ    R = ρL/A

What you're looking at

Electrons ricocheting through a lattice of ions, with their recent paths trailing behind them. Toggle the field off and on. The change in each individual trajectory is almost invisible — the electrons keep moving fast and in random directions — but a slow systematic creep to the right appears, and that creep is the current. The animation exaggerates the drift by about ten orders of magnitude; the readout gives the honest number.

Try this

Set copper, 1.0 A, 1.0 mm², which is Beiser's Example 10.2. The drift velocity is 7.4 × 10−5 m/s — 0.074 mm/s, so an individual electron would take about four hours to cross a one-metre wire. Yet a lamp lights instantly when you throw the switch, because the field that starts every electron drifting propagates down the wire at close to the speed of light. The electrons were already there; they just all started shuffling at once.

Now look at the ratio vF/vd, which is around 1010. Passing a current through a metal perturbs the electron velocities by one part in ten billion. This is why Ohm's law is linear over such an enormous range: you are always in the regime of infinitesimal perturbation.

Then read the mean free path for copper — 38 nm, Example 10.3, which is about 150 ion spacings. That number should be startling. A perfect periodic lattice does not scatter an electron wave at all (that is what Bloch's theorem says, and what the last card of this chapter is about); an electron only scatters off a defect, or off an ion that thermal vibration has knocked out of place. Resistance is not caused by the lattice. It is caused by the lattice being imperfect.

Switch to sodium and iron. Sodium has a third the free electrons of copper but a resistivity only 2.7 times higher; iron has twice copper's electron density and 5.6 times its resistivity. Iron's electrons travel only about 4 nm between collisions against copper's 38, and it is that, not the carrier count, that makes it the worse conductor.

Where Ohm's law actually comes from

Between collisions an electron accelerates at eE/m for an average time τ = λ/vF, picking up a drift eEτ/m before the next collision randomises its direction again. So vd ∝ E, hence I ∝ E ∝ V — and the constant of proportionality contains only material properties. Note which velocity appears in τ: the Fermi velocity, not a thermal one. Only electrons at the top of the distribution have empty states to scatter into, so only they matter, and their speed is set by the exclusion principle rather than by temperature. Drude got the right form of Ohm's law in 1900 using the Maxwell–Boltzmann speed instead, and his resistivities came out about ten times too large.

The catch

Beiser's Example 10.2 prints the drift velocity as 7.4 × 10−4 m/s. Substituting his own figures — n = 8.5 × 1028 m−3, A = 10−6 m², I = 1.0 A — gives 1/(1.36 × 104) = 7.4 × 10−5 m/s. The exponent appears to have slipped by one; his following sentence, "usually less than 1 mm/s", is satisfied either way. The value computed above is the arithmetic one.

The model also treats every metal as having exactly one free electron per atom, which is roughly right for the monovalent metals and wrong for aluminium (three) and iron. The densities used here are measured ones, not counted ones.

How a Level Becomes a Band Sec. 10.6

Bring two atoms together and their identical levels split in two. Bring N together and you get exactly N levels — and once N is more than about twenty, they are spread over a width that stops depending on N entirely. That last sentence is the whole of band theory.

Ej = E0 + 2t cos(jπ/(N+1)),   j = 1 … N   →   band width = 4t

What you're looking at

Above: the actual energy levels of a chain of N identical atoms, each coupled to its neighbours with strength t. Those levels are not sketched — the eigenvalues of that Hamiltonian have the closed form E0 + 2t cos(jπ/(N+1)), and that is what is plotted. Two atoms give the bonding/antibonding pair of Chapter 8. Five give five levels. A hundred give something your eye already reads as a continuous band.

Below: how the band widths depend on how close the atoms are, which is Beiser's Fig. 10.20 computed rather than drawn. The outer level (sodium's 3s) has a diffuse wave function, so it starts broadening while the atoms are still far apart. The inner level (2p) is tucked close to the nucleus and stays a sharp line until the atoms are practically touching. Both bands widen exponentially as the atoms approach, because t is an overlap integral and overlap of exponential tails is exponential.

Try this

Set the distance to 0.367 nm, the observed spacing in solid sodium, and step N up from 2. Watch the readout line for band width. At N = 2 you have just the bonding/antibonding pair and the width is only half its eventual value, but by N = 10 it is within 3 percent of the limit and by N = 20 within 1 percent. After that the width is simply fixed, and every further atom you add does nothing but subdivide it. Extrapolate that to a real crystal, as the last readout line does: a mole of sodium atoms gives a 3s band a couple of eV wide containing 6 × 1023 levels, so the spacing between them is around 10−24 eV. No measurement will ever resolve that, and no thermal or electrical perturbation will ever fail to bridge it. The band is a continuum for every purpose you will ever have.

Now drag the distance out to 0.8 nm and back. The inner band collapses to a line long before the outer one does. This is why chemistry and conduction are the business of valence electrons only: the inner shells of neighbouring atoms simply never find out about each other.

Why this decides conductor from insulator

An s level holds 2 electrons, so an s band built from N atoms holds 2N. Sodium brings one 3s electron per atom, so its 3s band is half full and the Fermi level sits in the middle of it — there are empty states an infinitesimal energy above the occupied ones, an applied field can accelerate electrons into them, and sodium conducts. Magnesium brings two, which ought to fill its 3s band exactly; magnesium is nevertheless a metal because its 3s and 3p bands overlap into one band holding 8N, filled to a quarter. Everything about electrical behaviour follows from counting: how many electrons, how many states, and whether the last electron lands in the middle of a band or at the top of one.

The catch

The chain here is one-dimensional and each atom carries a single orbital, which is the simplest system that shows the effect honestly. Real bands in three dimensions have structure — their width depends on direction, and bands built from different atomic levels cross and hybridise, which is what Fig. 10.23 shows happening to carbon's 2s and 2p. The exponential form t = t0e−(a−a0)/δ used for the overlap is a model; the level count, the closed-form eigenvalues and the N-independence of the width are exact.

Conductor, Semiconductor, Insulator — It Is All One Number Sec. 10.6

Diamond and silicon have the same crystal structure and the same kind of bonding. One is a jeweller's insulator, the other runs the world's computers. The difference is 4.4 eV.

ni = 2(2πkT/h²)3/2(m*em*h)3/4 e−Eg/2kT

What you're looking at

Above: the two bands, with the Fermi–Dirac occupancy f(ε) = 1/(e(ε−εF)/kT + 1) plotted sideways against them on a logarithmic scale. Now switch to diamond and read the last line. At 300 K the carrier density is around 10−27 cm−3 — which is to say that a diamond the size of the Earth would contain roughly one thermally excited conduction electron. That is what a 5.5 eV gap buys you when kT is 0.026 eV. The exponential does not care that the gap is only about 200 times kT; e−106 is a very small number.

Keep silicon and drag the temperature from 300 K to 600 K. The carrier density rises by about five orders of magnitude. This is why semiconductor devices need heatsinks and why they fail thermally rather than gracefully: the intrinsic carriers eventually swamp the doping, and the junction stops being a junction. It is also why germanium, with its smaller gap, was abandoned for silicon.

Why the gap is Eg/2 in the exponent

Because the Fermi level sits in the middle. An electron reaching the conduction-band edge has climbed from εF at the gap's centre, a distance Eg/2, and the Boltzmann tail of the Fermi–Dirac function then gives e−Eg/2kT. The factor of two is worth a factor of 1010 in silicon, so it is not a detail.

The catch

The effective masses are the one thing here that has to be supplied rather than derived — they encode the curvature of the real bands, which the free-electron picture cannot give you. The readout shows what happens if you use m* = m instead: silicon is then off by only 10 percent, but indium antimonide, whose electrons have an effective mass of 0.014m, is off by a factor of 47. That is a useful warning about how far the free-electron model can be pushed.

And the gap itself is not constant: it shrinks with temperature (silicon's falls from 1.17 eV at 0 K to 1.11 at 300 K) because the lattice expands and the atoms vibrate. Holding it fixed, as here, slightly understates the high-temperature carrier density.

Doping and the p–n Junction Sec. 10.6–10.7

Add one foreign atom per billion and the conductivity changes by orders of magnitude. Put two differently doped pieces together and you get a device that passes current one way and not the other — which is, in the end, what the whole of electronics is built from.

What you're looking at

Above: the band edges bending across the junction. Try this

Take silicon at 1014 cm−3 doping — that is one donor per 5 × 108 silicon atoms, a purity level that is itself a triumph of chemical engineering. The electron density jumps from 1010 to 1014, the hole density falls to about 106 (because np = ni² is fixed), and the conductivity rises by four orders of magnitude. That is the point of doping: it does not just add carriers, it makes the material's behaviour depend on something you control instead of on the temperature.

Now do the same on germanium and watch what fails. Germanium's intrinsic density is around 1013–1014 cm−3, so a doping of 1014 barely beats the carriers that thermal energy is producing for free. Beiser's remark that one donor per 109 host atoms raises conductivity by nearly 103 holds for silicon — one per 109 gives Nd = 5 × 1013, and the readout confirms a factor of about 3400. Run the same sum on germanium and the factor is 2.3. The doping does essentially nothing, because the thermal background has already swamped it. This is the real reason germanium lost.

Look at the "mV per decade" figure on the I–V plot — 59.5 mV at 300 K, and it depends on nothing but kT/e. Every decade of diode current costs the same 60 mV. It is the most-used number in analogue circuit design, and it is on this plot for the same reason it is in the design manuals: ln(10) kT/e.

Now raise the temperature to 400 K and watch that figure grow to 79 mV, while the whole curve shifts. A diode is a rather good thermometer and a rather poor voltage reference, and both facts are the same fact.

Why the junction rectifies

Two currents cross the junction. The thermal current it comes from electron–hole pairs generated by heat in the depletion region and swept across by the built-in field; it depends on temperature but not on the applied voltage, because every carrier generated there gets swept across regardless. The recombination current ir comes from majority carriers that have enough energy to climb the barrier, and that is exponentially sensitive to the barrier height, hence to the bias. At zero bias they cancel exactly. Forward bias multiplies ir by eeV/kT and leaves it alone; reverse bias kills ir and leaves it alone. Subtract, and you have the diode equation with I0 = it.

The catch

The band diagram here is schematic in its shape — a real depletion region has a parabolic potential profile whose width depends on the doping as 1/√Nd — though the barrier height, the carrier densities and the I–V curve are all computed. The diode equation also assumes ideal injection; real diodes carry an ideality factor between 1 and 2, and at high forward bias the series resistance of the bulk material takes over and the current becomes linear again. Complete ionization of the dopants is assumed, which fails below about 100 K when the donor electrons freeze back onto their donors.

Brillouin Zones, and Where the Gap Actually Comes From Sec. 10.8

The previous account built bands by bringing atoms together. Here is an entirely different one that gets the same answer: an electron in a periodic lattice is a wave, and at certain wavelengths the lattice Bragg-reflects it. Those wavelengths become forbidden.

What you're looking at

Top: E against k. The dashed grey line is the free-electron parabola E = ℏ²k²/2m. The coloured curves are the real answer, obtained by diagonalising the electron's Hamiltonian in a basis of nine plane waves coupled by the periodic potential — not sketched, computed, by bisection on the Sturm sequence of the resulting tridiagonal matrix. Away from the zone boundaries the two agree to the width of the line. At k = ±nπ/a the curve splits, and the shaded strips are energies no electron in this crystal can have.

Middle: the same boundaries in two dimensions. The first Brillouin zone is the square of k-values that can avoid Bragg reflection from the vertical and horizontal rows of ions; the second is the diamond of larger k that still avoids reflection from the diagonal rows. The black circle is the Fermi circle, whose radius is set by how many electrons you put in.

Bottom: why there is a gap at all. At exactly k = π/a the electron waves are Bragg-reflected back on themselves, and the only solutions are standing waves. There are two of them with the same wavelength: sin(πx/a), which has nodes at the ions, and cos(πx/a), which has antinodes there. The cosine keeps its charge near the positive ions where the potential energy is lowest; the sine keeps it between them, where it is highest. Same k, different energy — and the difference between those two energies is the gap.

Try this

Drag |U| from 0 upward and watch the gaps open. At U = 0 the curve is exactly the free-electron parabola folded onto itself. The readout checks the computed first gap against 2|U|, which is what the two-state secular equation predicts — and the numerical diagonalisation agrees, which is a good sign that the machinery is doing what it claims. Then look at the line for the next boundary, k = 2π/a. With only the first Fourier component of the lattice potential present, the bands there are coupled only at second order — through an intermediate plane wave — so that gap goes as |U|²/E and is dramatically narrower. At |U| = 0.8 eV the first gap is 1.60 eV and the second only 0.076 eV, twenty times narrower. Push |U| up to 3 and the second gap grows to about 1.0 eV — the second-order term scales as the square, so it catches up fast once the potential is strong.

Set a = 0.300 nm and read the wavelength at the zone boundary: λ = 2a = 0.600 nm. That is the Bragg condition nλ = 2a sinθ at normal incidence. An electron with that wavelength is diffracted by the crystal exactly as an X-ray of that wavelength would be — it is the same equation from Chapter 2, doing a completely different job.

Now drag electrons per atom. At 1 electron per atom the Fermi circle sits comfortably inside the first zone, nowhere near a boundary, and the electrons never discover that the lattice exists — a monovalent metal. Push toward 2 and the circle reaches the zone faces; past that it would have to spill into the second zone, and whether it can depends on whether the gap is bigger than the energy difference between the zone corner and the second-zone bottom. That single question is the difference between a divalent metal and an insulator.

The two stories are the same story

Section 10.6 built bands by broadening atomic levels; this section builds them by diffracting free electrons. They are opposite limits of one problem — tight binding starts from strong lattice potential and weak overlap, nearly-free-electron theory starts from weak potential and strong delocalisation — and they meet in the middle. The number of states in a band comes out the same either way: 2N for an s band, because there are exactly N allowed k values in a Brillouin zone for N atoms and two spins for each. That is not a coincidence but a counting theorem, and it is the reason both pictures predict the same conductors.

The catch

The lattice is one-dimensional for the E–k plot and a two-dimensional square for the zone picture; real zones are polyhedra (Fig. 10.42) and real gaps depend on direction, which is why a material can have a gap along one axis and none along another and end up a metal anyway. |U| here is a single number standing in for the whole Fourier series of the crystal potential. And the electron's mass near a band edge is not m: the curvature of E(k) is different there, and the useful bookkeeping is to keep the free-electron formulas but replace m with an effective mass m* — 1.01m in copper, 0.85m in zinc, but 28m in nickel.

Superconductivity Secs. 10.9–10.10

Not very little resistance — none. Currents set up in superconducting rings have been watched for years without measurable decay. The explanation took forty-six years to find, and it turns on electrons attracting each other.

What you're looking at

What a Cooper pair is

An electron moving through the lattice drags the positive ions slightly toward it. They are heavy and respond slowly, so the region of enhanced positive charge persists after the electron has gone — and a second electron, arriving later, is attracted to it. The net effect is an attraction between the two electrons, mediated by the lattice, and if it beats their Coulomb repulsion they form a bound pair. The pair's members move in opposite directions and may be as much as a micrometre apart, which is thousands of lattice spacings; "pair" does not mean "adjacent".

Two electrons have spin ½ each, so a pair with opposite spins has spin 0. That makes Cooper pairs bosons, and any number of bosons may occupy the same state. All the pairs in a superconductor drop into one collective ground state described by a single wave function the size of the sample. To scatter one electron now means promoting the entire condensate, which costs far more than a lattice defect can supply — so nothing scatters, and the resistance is not small but exactly zero.

Try this

Take mercury, Tc = 4.15 K. The gap is 1.26 meV — about 10−3 eV, as Beiser says, which is four orders of magnitude below a chemical bond. Read the photon line: breaking a pair takes microwave radiation of wavelength around a millimetre, and that is exactly how gaps are measured. Shine microwaves on a superconductor and sweep the frequency; absorption switches on abruptly at hν = Eg.

Then read the last line: room-temperature kT is roughly twenty times the gap. Superconductivity is cryogenic for the plainest possible reason — the binding is so feeble that ordinary thermal energy tears the pairs apart instantly.

Drag the temperature up toward Tc. The gap holds up remarkably well to about 0.4 Tc and then collapses. It is a cooperative effect: breaking some pairs frees electrons that interfere with the remaining ones, which makes the next pairs easier to break, which is the runaway that ends at Tc.

Now compare lead with copper — except copper is not in the list, because copper does not superconduct at any temperature yet reached. This is the beautiful inversion at the heart of the theory. Copper and silver are superb ordinary conductors because their lattices barely vibrate and barely scatter electrons; and that same stiffness means the lattice cannot deform enough to mediate pairing. Lead, mercury and tin are mediocre conductors at room temperature because their lattices are floppy — and that floppiness is precisely what makes them superconductors when cooled. Good conductors make bad superconductors, and the reason is one property read two ways.

Switch to Nb₃Sn and then YBa₂Cu₃O₇. Look at the field axis: the critical fields jump from hundredths of a tesla to tens of teslas. Type II materials admit magnetic flux in quantised filaments while staying superconducting elsewhere, which is why they survive fields that would destroy a type I superconductor a thousand times over — and why every MRI scanner and every particle accelerator magnet is wound with them.

The flux quantum

Flux threading a superconducting ring is trapped, because any change in it would induce a current that opposes the change and there is no resistance to damp that current. Since the pair wave function must come back to itself around the ring, the trapped flux is quantised in units of Φ0 = h/2e = 2.068 × 10−15 T·m². The 2e is the experimental fingerprint of pairing: it was measured before anyone could explain it, and it is why we know the carriers have twice the electron charge. That quantum is tiny — through a square-millimetre loop it corresponds to a field of 2 × 10−9 T — which is what makes a SQUID the most sensitive magnetometer that exists, able to detect the magnetic field of a thought.

The catch

The parabolic Bc(T) is an empirical fit, good to a few percent but not exact. The BCS gap curve computed here assumes weak coupling; lead and mercury are strong-coupling superconductors whose measured 2Δ(0)/kTc runs to 4.3 rather than 3.53, so the universal curve is a first approximation for them.

Most importantly, the last two materials in the list are not explained by any of this. The cuprate superconductors above 90 K are type II and have gaps, but the pairing is not the phonon-mediated mechanism described here — forty years after their discovery, the mechanism remains unsettled. Everything on this card is solid physics for the elements; for the ceramics it is a description, not an explanation.

Chapter Eleven

Nuclear Structure

Everything up to here has been about electrons. The nucleus has been a point charge that holds them in place. It is now the subject: a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom, four hundred trillion times denser than lead, held together by a force so strong it overcomes an electrical repulsion of forty newtons between neighbouring protons — and understood, even today, only through models that contradict each other and both work.

The atomic masses in this chapter are Beiser's own appendix table, 378 nuclides with their measured masses, natural abundances and stability. Every binding energy below is computed from those masses, not looked up.

How Big, How Dense, How Repulsive Sec. 11.2, Examples 11.1–11.2

One formula — R = 1.2 A1/3 fm — contains the single most important fact about nuclear matter: it is incompressible. Every nucleus has the same density, and that density is absurd.

R ≈ 1.2 A1/3 fm   →   ρ = m/(4/3)πR³ = constant

What you're looking at

Above: the nucleons of a nucleus of mass number A, packed at the density the formula implies, with a dashed circle showing uranium for scale. Drag A from 1 to 250 and notice how little the radius changes: 250 nucleons fit inside a sphere only about 6.3 times the radius of one. That is the whole content of R ∝ A1/3 — volume proportional to the number of nucleons, so each nucleon occupies its own fixed 7.2 fm³ no matter what it is next to.

Below: the nucleon density plotted against distance from the centre, the quantity that electron-scattering experiments actually measure. Two things stand out, and both are consequences of that same incompressibility. The interior is flat — a nucleon deep inside a gold nucleus has the same neighbours as one inside carbon. And the skin is the same thickness in every nucleus, about 2.4 fm from 90 percent density down to 10 percent, whether the nucleus is small or large. R = 1.2 A1/3 is defined as where the density falls to half its central value.

Try this

Set A = 12, Beiser's Example 11.1. The radius is 2.7 fm and the density comes out at about 2.3 × 1017 kg/m³. Now drag A anywhere you like and watch that number not move. The readout converts it: roughly four billion tonnes per cubic inch. A teaspoon of nuclear matter outweighs every building in a large city.

Compare it with the neutron-star line. A neutron star packs 1.4 solar masses into a 10 km ball, reaching about three times nuclear density — a star is what you get when gravity crushes ordinary matter until the exclusion principle among nucleons is the only thing left holding it up. The two numbers being within a factor of a few is not a coincidence: a neutron star is a nucleus, 1019 metres too big and held together by gravity instead of the strong force.

Now the second slider, Beiser's Example 11.2. At 2.4 fm — roughly the separation of two protons inside a nucleus — the Coulomb repulsion is 40 newtons, about 9 pounds. That is a force you could feel in your hand, acting on a particle weighing 1.7 × 10−27 kg. Read the acceleration it implies. Whatever holds the nucleus together against that has to be, at these distances, in a completely different class from electromagnetism.

Why the density is constant, and why it matters

If nuclear forces were long-ranged like gravity or electricity, every nucleon would attract every other, the binding energy would go as A², and nuclei would collapse to smaller and smaller volumes as they got heavier. They do not. Constant density means each nucleon interacts with only its immediate neighbours — the force saturates, exactly as chemical bonds in a liquid do. That single observation is the origin of the liquid-drop model two cards from here, and of the volume term a1A that dominates the binding energy.

The catch

The picture above shows nucleons as little spheres at rest in fixed positions. They are neither. A nucleon confined to a few fermi has, by the uncertainty principle, a momentum corresponding to tens of MeV of kinetic energy — they are moving at a substantial fraction of c, and the nucleus is better thought of as a seething Fermi gas than as a bag of marbles. The packing here is for scale, not for structure.

R = 1.2 A1/3 also breaks down at the light end. There is no meaningful radius for A = 1, and for deuterium the two nucleons spend most of their time outside the range of their own attraction — the deuteron is enormous and barely bound, which is why its binding energy per nucleon is 1.1 MeV against carbon's 7.7.

Nuclear Spin, the Magneton, and How an MRI Works Sec. 11.2, Example 11.3

Protons and neutrons are fermions with spin ½, so they have magnetic moments — tiny ones, 1836 times smaller than an electron's. Putting them in a strong field splits their levels by about a millionth of an electronvolt, and that ridiculous little splitting is the basis of a hospital imaging machine.

μN = eℏ/2mp    ΔE = 2μzB    νL = ΔE/h

What you're looking at

The magnetic energy of a nucleus as the field is turned up. A nucleus of spin I has 2I + 1 orientations, and each one has a different energy in a field — the same Zeeman splitting as in Chapter 6, but on the nuclear scale. A spin-½ nucleus gives two levels, spin 1 gives three, spin 3/2 gives four. All the lines are straight because the energy is simply −μzB.

Try this

Take the proton at 1.000 T, Beiser's Example 11.3. ΔE is 1.761 × 10−7 eV and the Larmor frequency is 42.58 MHz. That frequency per tesla is the number every MRI physicist knows by heart, and the field in a clinical scanner is 1.5 or 3 T, so the radio transmitter runs at 64 or 128 MHz — just below the FM broadcast band. Radio waves of that wavelength pass through a human being as though they were not there, which is the entire reason this works as a medical imaging method and X-rays do not.

Now select the neutron. It has no charge at all, yet it has a magnetic moment — and the moment points opposite to its spin, where the proton's points along. Neither fact makes any sense for a structureless particle. Both are direct evidence that nucleons have charged constituents inside them, which is Chapter 13's subject and which no one suspected when these moments were first measured.

Look at the moment of the proton: 2.793 μN, not 1. A structureless spin-½ particle of charge e should have a moment of exactly one magneton — the electron's, in Bohr magnetons, is 1.001. The proton is off by a factor of nearly three. That anomaly, and the neutron's −1.913, were measured by Otto Stern in the 1930s and were the first hard evidence that the nucleons are composite.

Finally, read the last line. At room temperature kT is about 150,000 times the splitting, so thermal motion very nearly randomises the spins: only a few nuclei per million end up in the lower state. An MRI image is built from that few-in-a-million excess — which is why scanners need enormous fields and why a hydrogen-rich sample (i.e. water, i.e. you) is the only thing with enough nuclei to make it work.

Why the nuclear magneton is so small

μN = eℏ/2mp differs from the Bohr magneton only in carrying the proton mass instead of the electron mass, so it is smaller by 1836. Nuclear magnetism is therefore about two thousand times weaker than atomic magnetism, which is why the magnetism of ordinary materials is entirely an electron affair and why nuclear resonance needs fields measured in teslas rather than millitesla.

The catch

The moments here are measured values, not predictions — no model gets them right from first principles, and the shell model manages only to bracket them (the Schmidt limits). Also note that not every nucleus is usable: any nucleus with even Z and even N has spin 0 and no moment at all, which rules out 12C, 16O and 40Ca — the three commonest nuclei in your body after hydrogen. Carbon imaging has to use 13C, which is 1.1 percent abundant and has a sixth of the proton's sensitivity, and that is why MRI is a hydrogen technique.

Weigh a nucleus and it comes out lighter than its parts. The missing mass is the binding energy, and plotting it against mass number produces a curve with a peak in the middle — which is why stars shine, why bombs work, and why there is iron in your blood.

Where the mass goes

It does not "convert to energy" in any mysterious way. The bound system has less energy than its separated parts, and since energy has mass, it weighs less. The same is true of a hydrogen atom, whose 13.6 eV binding makes it lighter than a free proton and electron by 1.5 × 10−8 u — far too small to weigh. Nuclear binding is a million times larger, which pushes the mass defect up to about one part in 1000 and makes it the easiest thing in nuclear physics to measure.

The catch

The formula uses atomic masses, including electrons, on both sides — Z hydrogen atoms against one AZX atom — so the Z electron masses cancel. This is a convenience, not an approximation, but it is why you must use m(1H) = 1.007825 u and never the bare proton mass 1.007276 u. Substituting the proton mass would be an error of 0.000549 u per proton, or half an MeV per proton, which for uranium is 45 MeV.

Strictly the electron binding energies do not quite cancel either, since they differ between a hydrogen atom and a heavy atom. For uranium the discrepancy is of order 0.5 MeV against a binding energy of 1800 — below the resolution of anything on this page, but not zero.

The Liquid-Drop Model Sec. 11.5, Example 11.6

Treat the nucleus as a drop of incompressible fluid. Four terms — volume, surface, electrostatic, and a quantum correction for having unequal numbers of neutrons and protons — reproduce the binding energy of every nucleus to a fraction of a percent. The five coefficients are the only things fitted.

Eb = a1A − a2A2/3 − a3Z(Z−1)/A1/3 − a4(A−2Z)²/A ± a5/A3/4

Where each term comes from

Volume. If every nucleon had 12 neighbours and each bond were worth U, the total would be 6AU — proportional to A. This is the term that exists only because the force saturates. It is also the largest, and on its own it would make the binding energy per nucleon a flat 14 MeV line.

Surface. Nucleons on the outside have fewer neighbours, and their number goes as the surface area, 4πR² ∝ A2/3. Subtracting it turns the flat line into a rising one, because the surface fraction falls as nuclei get bigger. It is exactly surface tension, and it is why nuclei are spherical.

Coulomb. The Z(Z−1)/2 proton pairs each contribute e²/4πε0r, and ⟨1/r⟩ ∝ 1/R ∝ A−1/3. This term grows roughly as Z²/A1/3, faster than A, so it eventually overwhelms everything — and that is what pulls the right-hand end of the curve down and puts a limit on how big a nucleus can be.

Asymmetry. This one is not fluid mechanics at all; it is the exclusion principle. Neutrons and protons fill separate ladders of levels. Converting protons into neutrons past the point of equality means stacking them into higher levels, and the cost goes as (N−Z)²/A. It is the term that keeps light nuclei at N ≈ Z.

Pairing. Also not fluid mechanics: nucleons of the same kind pair off with opposite spins, so even-even nuclei are extra bound and odd-odd nuclei extra loose. This is the term that produces the zigzag on the next card, and the reason 60 percent of the stable nuclides in the table are even-even (156 of 261) while just four are odd-odd — 2H, 6Li, 10B and 14N, to which Beiser adds the exotic 180Ta.

Try this

With Beiser's coefficients the mean error over every stable nuclide with A ≥ 16 is well under one percent — from five numbers. Set Z = 30, A = 64 on the previous card, Example 11.6: measured 559.1 MeV, formula 561.7, an error of 0.5 percent.

Now drag a₃ (Coulomb) to zero. The curve stops turning over and rises forever, and the mean error explodes. Without proton repulsion there would be no heaviest element, no fission, and no limit to the periodic table. Put it back and drag a₂ (surface) to zero instead: now the curve is flat from the start, the light nuclei are massively over-bound, and the rise that makes fusion profitable disappears. The shape of the curve — and therefore the existence of both fusion and fission — comes from the competition between exactly those two terms.

Try a₄ (asymmetry) to zero and watch the mean error rise sharply even though the general shape survives. The asymmetry term is worth only a couple of MeV per nucleon, but it is what decides which isobar at each A is the stable one, which is the subject of the next card.

Where the model fails, and why that is the interesting part

The lower plot shows measured minus predicted binding energy against neutron number. If the model were complete this would be structureless noise. It is not. The gold line is the local trend, and the red points — N = 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 — sit consistently above it, by between half an MeV and three MeV. That is small against a thousand-MeV binding energy, which is why the liquid drop is such a good model. But it is systematic, and it is always in the same direction. Those are the magic numbers, and a smooth fluid model has no way to produce them. The residuals of a good model are where the next model comes from, and in this case they point straight at the shell structure of the card after next.

The catch

The coefficients are fitted, not derived — Beiser gives one set and notes that others have been proposed. Different fitting sets and slightly different functional forms (Z² instead of Z(Z−1), A−1/2 instead of A−3/4 in the pairing term) all do about as well, so no single number here should be read as a measured physical constant.

The formula also fails badly for A < 16, which is why the error figure above excludes them: light nuclei have too few nucleons for "volume" and "surface" to be distinguishable, and their binding is dominated by shell effects and by alpha-particle clustering. The model is a statistical description, and statistics needs a crowd.

The Valley of Stability Sec. 11.3, Example 11.7

Of the thousands of possible combinations of neutrons and protons, fewer than 300 are stable, and they form a narrow ridge that curves away from N = Z as it climbs. The liquid-drop formula predicts where that ridge runs — and differentiating it gives the stable isobar at any mass number.

Now step through odd values of A. Every one gives a single clean parabola with one minimum and, almost always, exactly one stable isobar. Then step through even values and watch the curve split into two interleaved parabolas — one for even-even nuclei sitting high, one for odd-odd sitting low, separated by twice the pairing term. Drag A from 6 to 238 and watch the red prediction curve on the chart peel away from the N = Z line. At A = 16 the stable nucleus has 8 neutrons and 8 protons; at A = 208 it has 126 and 82. Extra neutrons add attraction without adding repulsion, so as the Coulomb term grows the nucleus has to buy stability with a neutron excess — paying for it in asymmetry energy. The whole shape of the valley is those two terms bargaining.

Take A = 209, bismuth. That is the end of the road: no nucleus heavier than 209Bi is stable, because by then the Coulomb term has grown faster than any amount of neutron excess can compensate. Everything above it alpha-decays its way back down.

Why there is a valley at all

Two terms depend on Z at fixed A and they pull in opposite directions. The asymmetry term −a4(A−2Z)²/A is minimised at Z = A/2 and wants equal numbers. The Coulomb term −a3Z(Z−1)/A1/3 always wants fewer protons. Their sum is a parabola in Z, its minimum sits below A/2, and the heavier the nucleus the further below. Everything about the chart of the nuclides — its curve, its narrowness, its abrupt end — falls out of that one competition.

The catch

The formula is smooth and the real world is not. It cannot know that 4He, 16O and 208Pb are doubly magic and unusually bound, and it will happily predict a minimum at a Z where the real nucleus is radioactive. Near the magic numbers the measured points (red) and the formula (teal) visibly disagree on the lower plot.

Beta stability is also not the same as stability. Several nuclides sit at the bottom of their isobar parabola and are still radioactive, because they can alpha-decay or fission instead — routes that change A and so do not appear on this slice at all. And "stable" here means Beiser's table; a few of those, like 209Bi itself, have since been found to decay with half-lives of 1019 years, which is a distinction without a practical difference.

The Shell Model and the Magic Numbers Sec. 11.6

Nuclei with 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 or 126 nucleons of a kind are conspicuously more stable and more abundant than their neighbours. Reproducing that list defeated everyone for twenty years, until Goeppert-Mayer and Jensen added one ingredient.

The one ingredient that was missing

Atoms have spin–orbit coupling too, and in atoms it is a small correction — the sodium D lines are split by 0.6 nm out of 589, about one part in a thousand. In nuclei the same interaction is enormous, comparable to the level spacing itself. When it is that strong, a level of high ℓ splits so far that the j = ℓ + ½ member drops right out of its own group and into the one below. Watch the 5g9/2 level (capacity 10) do exactly this as you drag the slider: it falls out of its shell and joins the one beneath, turning what would have been a closure at 40 into one at 50. The same manoeuvre by 6h11/2 makes 82, and by 7i13/2 makes 126.

That is the whole trick, and it is worth appreciating how unsatisfying it looked at the time. There was no derivation — the strength was chosen to fit the data, and the justification came only later from the structure of the nucleon–nucleon force. Goeppert-Mayer and Jensen shared the 1963 Nobel Prize for what was, in essence, a very good guess supported by a great deal of evidence.

Two models that contradict each other and both work

The liquid drop says nucleons collide constantly with their neighbours, like molecules in a fluid. The shell model says each nucleon orbits independently in an average field, without colliding at all. They cannot both be literally true — and the reconciliation is the exclusion principle again. In a nucleus in its ground state every low-lying level is full. For one nucleon to lose energy in a collision, another must gain it, and the receiving nucleon has nowhere to go: every state it could be scattered into is occupied. So collisions are forbidden, even in a tightly packed nucleus, and the independent-particle picture is justified precisely by how crowded the nucleus is. The same argument, made about electrons, is why the free-electron model of a metal works in Chapter 10.

The catch

The level energies plotted here are schematic — the real ordering depends on A and on the exact potential, and the diagram is drawn for a mid-weight nucleus. Beiser's own figure is explicitly "not to scale", and his n-labelling differs from the standard spectroscopic one (he writes 2p where most references write 1p). What is not schematic is the capacities, the ordering of closures, and the resulting magic numbers.

The model also assumes a spherical potential, which fails badly for nuclei in between the magic numbers: those are deformed, sometimes into shapes twice as long as they are wide, and describing them needs the collective model of Aage Bohr and Mottelson, which grafts liquid-drop rotations and vibrations onto shell-model states.

Yukawa: Weighing a Particle Nobody Had Seen Sec. 11.7

In 1935 Yukawa argued that if nucleons hold each other by tossing a particle back and forth, then the range of the force fixes the mass of the particle. He knew the range. He got a mass, predicted a particle, and twelve years later it turned up.

ΔE Δt ≈ ℏ,   ΔE = mc²,   Δt = r/c  →  m ≈ ℏ/rc

The argument

A nucleon emits a pion and does not get lighter. That is a straightforward violation of energy conservation — except that the uncertainty principle permits a violation of size ΔE provided it is repaired within a time ℏ/ΔE, which is short enough that no measurement could in principle detect it. The pion is virtual: it exists only on borrowed energy, and the loan must be repaid before anyone can check the books.

That sets the range. The particle can travel at most c × Δt = ℏc/mc² before it has to be reabsorbed. A heavy exchanged particle gives a short-range force; a light one a long-range force; a massless one an infinite-range force. Run the logic backwards from the observed 1.7 fm range of the nuclear force and you get a mass of a couple of hundred electron masses — between the electron and the nucleon, hence "meson", the middle-weight.

The exponential is the entire difference between a nuclear force and an electromagnetic one, and it comes from the single fact that the pion has mass. The photon is massless, so the Coulomb force has infinite range, and the 1/r² law you learned in school is a statement about the photon's rest mass being exactly zero. The same logic covers the weak force: the W and Z weigh about 90 GeV, and their range is accordingly 0.002 fm, which is why the weak interaction appears to act only on contact.

Note the conversion in the last readout line: ℏc = 197 MeV·fm. Range in fermis times mass in MeV is always about 200. It is the most-used number in particle physics and it is nothing but this argument, written once.

The sequel, which is a good story

In 1936 Carl Anderson found a particle in cosmic rays with almost exactly the predicted mass, and everyone concluded Yukawa had been vindicated. He had not: that particle passed straight through nuclei without interacting strongly, which was precisely the wrong behaviour for the carrier of the strong force. It was the muon — a heavy electron, nothing to do with nuclei at all. The real pion was found by Powell in 1947, decaying into the muon that had been confusing everybody for a decade. Yukawa got the Nobel Prize in 1949, the first awarded to a Japanese scientist.

The catch

The estimate is dimensional analysis, not a calculation: it assumes the pion travels at c (it does not), ignores its kinetic energy entirely, and uses ΔEΔt ≈ ℏ rather than ℏ/2. Getting within 17 percent from that is as much luck as insight, and the argument establishes the scale rather than the value.

More deeply, pion exchange is no longer the fundamental story. The strong interaction is now understood as acting between quarks via gluons, and the nuclear force between nucleons is a residual effect — a leakage, analogous to the van der Waals force between neutral atoms in Chapter 10. Pion exchange remains an excellent effective description at nuclear distances, in the same way that the van der Waals force remains an excellent description of argon without anyone pretending it is fundamental.

Chapter Twelve

Nuclear Transformations

The previous chapter asked what a nucleus is. This one asks what it does. Unstable nuclei change into other nuclei on a schedule nothing can alter — not heat, not pressure, not chemistry — and that indifference is what makes them clocks. The same physics splits uranium, lights the sun, and dates the Earth.

Masses, abundances and half-lives below all come from Beiser's own appendix table: 378 nuclides, 108 of them with a listed half-life. Every Q value, decay constant and activity is computed from those.

Five Kinds of Decay, and Which One a Nucleus Picks Secs. 12.1, 12.3, Example 12.1

A nucleus off the valley floor has a choice of routes back. Which routes are open is not a matter of opinion — it is arithmetic on measured masses, and the arithmetic picks out exactly one.

Finally, walk Z along a fixed A — try A = 40 and step Z from 18 to 20. Argon-40 has every route closed and calcium-40 does too, but potassium-40 sits between them with Q(β⁻) = 1.31 MeV and Q(EC) = 1.50 MeV both positive. It is the rare nuclide that decays in both directions at once — 89% one way, 11% the other — and it is the reason your own body is mildly radioactive, and the reason potassium–argon dating works at all.

Why Q > 0 is the whole rule

Radioactive decay is spontaneous, so it cannot borrow energy. If the products weigh more than the parent, the decay is simply not available, no matter how far from stability the nucleus sits. This is why some badly unbalanced nuclei are nonetheless stable: 50V and 180Ta are both odd-odd and both look precarious, but every route out of them is uphill.

One route is not shown here because it changes nothing on this chart: gamma decay. An excited nucleus drops to its ground state by emitting a photon, with Z and N unchanged. It is the exact nuclear analogue of an atom emitting light, at a million times the energy.

The catch

Q > 0 says a decay is allowed, not that it happens quickly. Alpha decay is energetically open for every nucleus above about A = 150, yet most of them are perfectly stable in practice because the tunnelling rate is unimaginably slow — the subject of the fourth card. 209Bi has a positive alpha Q and a half-life of 1019 years. "Allowed" and "observed" are separated here by many orders of magnitude.

The atomic-mass bookkeeping also has a trap. Using atomic masses, the Z electrons cancel automatically for β⁻ and for capture, but β⁺ leaves two electron masses uncancelled — hence the explicit 2me in that Q value. Forget it and every positron emitter looks 1.022 MeV more likely than it is.

Read the mean life line. It is 1.44× the half-life, never equal to it, and the factor is 1/ln2. The half-life is the median lifetime; the mean is dragged up by the long tail of nuclei that survive many half-lives. Both are used, in different fields, and confusing them is a 44% error.

Set the time to 2 and read the fraction left: 25%, not 0%. This is the single most common misunderstanding about half-lives. A nucleus does not age. Having survived one half-life, it faces exactly the same 50% chance over the next one as it did over the first — so two half-lives leave a quarter, three leave an eighth, and nothing ever fully decays.

Where the exponential comes from

Assume each nucleus has a constant probability λ per second of decaying. Then in a sample of N nuclei, dN = −λN dt, and integrating gives N = N0e−λt. That is the whole derivation. What is remarkable is not the mathematics but that λ really is constant: it does not depend on temperature, pressure, chemical state, or how long the nucleus has already existed. The decay is a quantum tunnelling event with no memory, and it is that memorylessness that makes radioactivity the most trustworthy clock in science.

The catch

The exponential law is statistical. It describes what a large ensemble does, not what any individual nucleus will do, and in a small sample the fluctuations are large — with 100 atoms you will see roughly 50 ± 5 decay in a half-life, not exactly 50. The law is also only exactly right when the daughter is stable; in a decay chain the intermediate members build up and then follow the parent's decay constant, which is the secular-equilibrium result Beiser uses in Example 12.6 to measure the half-life of 238U from its ratio to 234U.

And λ is not quite absolutely constant. Electron capture depends on the electron density at the nucleus, so its rate shifts by a fraction of a percent with chemical environment or extreme pressure — 7Be is the standard example. The effect is real, tiny, and irrelevant to everything on this page.

Why radiocarbon works at all

Cosmic-ray neutrons hit atmospheric nitrogen and make 14C, which oxidises into CO₂ and enters every living thing through photosynthesis and then the food chain. While an organism lives, its 14C is topped up to the atmospheric ratio. When it dies the topping-up stops and the clock starts. What makes the method work is that a 5,700-year half-life is absurdly short compared with the age of the Earth — every atom of it now present was made in the last few tens of thousands of years, and the world's entire stock is about 90 tonnes.

The catch

Every method above assumes two things that are not guaranteed: that the sample started with a known amount of parent and none of the daughter, and that neither has entered or left since. Argon is a gas and leaks from heated rock, so a K–Ar age can mean "when this last cooled" rather than "when this formed". Lead is mobile in groundwater. The isochron technique exists precisely to test these assumptions rather than assume them.

Radiocarbon has its own problem: the atmospheric 14C level has not been constant. It varies with solar activity and the Earth's magnetic field, it was diluted by fossil-fuel burning from about 1890 (the Suess effect), and it was roughly doubled by atmospheric bomb testing in the 1950s. Real radiocarbon dates are therefore calibrated against tree rings and corals rather than read straight off this curve — which is why published dates carry both a "radiocarbon age" and a calibrated one.

Why an alpha and not a proton

Because the alpha particle is so extraordinarily well bound — 28.3 MeV for four nucleons, 7.07 MeV each, far above its neighbours on the binding-energy curve. That binding is what makes Q positive. For 232U, emitting an alpha releases 5.4 MeV; emitting a proton would cost 6.1 MeV and emitting a 3He nucleus would cost 9.6 MeV. The alpha is the only fragment light enough to assemble and tightly bound enough to pay for its own escape.

The catch

The model above is deliberately crude and the readout says so: it assumes the alpha already exists as a unit inside the nucleus (the preformation probability is really 10−2 or so, not 1), it uses a sharp-edged potential where the real one has a diffuse surface, and it ignores angular momentum entirely. An alpha emitted with ℓ > 0 faces an extra centrifugal barrier and is hindered, sometimes by two or three decades — which is why Cf-251 sits so far off the line, and why Ac-227 does too, though for a different reason: it mostly beta-decays, so its tabulated half-life is not its alpha half-life at all.

What survives all that is the slope. The model reproduces how the half-life varies with Q across seventeen decades, which is the part that matters, while the absolute normalisation absorbs everything it left out.

Beta Decay and the Invention of the Neutrino Sec. 12.5

Beta decay appeared to violate conservation of energy, of momentum, and of angular momentum, all at once. Pauli's response in 1930 was to propose an undetectable particle. It took twenty-six years to find it.

n → p + e− + ν̄    N(KE) ∝ pE(Q−KE)²F(Z,E)

The scandal

A nucleus of definite mass turns into a daughter of definite mass and emits an electron. That is a two-body decay, and a two-body decay has exactly one possible electron energy — a sharp line, like the alpha particles of the previous card. What was measured instead is the broad smear above: electrons come out with every energy from zero up to a maximum, and the maximum is what the mass difference predicts. Only a handful of electrons get it. The rest of the energy is simply gone.

Two more failures came with it. The electron and the recoiling nucleus are almost never emitted back-to-back, so momentum does not balance. And a neutron (spin ½) turning into a proton (½) and an electron (½) cannot conserve angular momentum: you cannot make two halves out of one. Bohr was prepared to abandon energy conservation in the nucleus. Pauli instead wrote to a conference in Tübingen apologising for a "desperate remedy" — an unseen, uncharged, spin-½ particle of negligible mass carrying away the balance of all three.

What you're looking at

The black line marks where the single sharp peak would be if there were no neutrino. How it was finally caught

By running the reaction backwards. An antineutrino striking a proton can produce a neutron and a positron — inverse beta decay — and although the cross section is around 10−19 barns, a nuclear reactor produces enough antineutrinos to compensate. Reines and Cowan did it in 1956 beside the Savannah River reactor, detecting the positron annihilation and the neutron capture in coincidence. Pauli, who had bet a case of champagne that his particle would never be found, paid up.

The catch

The Fermi function used here is the simple non-relativistic form, which captures the Coulomb distortion's shape but not its exact magnitude for heavy nuclei; the real one is a relativistic expression involving gamma functions. The spectrum shape also assumes an "allowed" transition — decays where the electron and neutrino carry off no orbital angular momentum. Forbidden transitions have extra energy-dependent factors and visibly different shapes, which is how the degree of forbiddenness is measured.

And the physics has moved on in a way the chapter predates. Neutrinos do have mass — that is now established from oscillation experiments, which show that the three neutrino flavours mix — but oscillations measure mass differences, not masses. The absolute scale is still unknown, and the endpoint of this curve remains one of the few ways to get at it.

Cross Section: How Likely Is a Nuclear Reaction? Sec. 12.7, Examples 12.8–12.10

A nucleus presents a target area of about one barn. Some nuclei present a quarter of a million. The difference is not geometry — it is the wave nature of a slow neutron.

N = N0e−nσx,   λmfp = 1/nσ,   rate = φσn

What a cross section is

Not an area, despite the units. It is a probability dressed as an area: σ is defined so that a beam passing through a slab containing n nuclei per cubic metre is attenuated as e−nσx. The unit is the barn, 10−28 m², chosen because it is roughly the geometric cross-section of a nucleus — and named, according to the story, because to a slow neutron a large nucleus is as easy to hit as the side of a barn.

Try this

Take cadmium at 0.10 mm, Beiser's Example 12.8. About 67% of a thermal-neutron beam is absorbed by a sheet you could cut with scissors, and 0.41 mm stops 99%. The mean free path is 0.089 mm, Example 12.9. This is why cadmium and boron are what reactor control rods are made of: a few millimetres of them is an absolute wall to slow neutrons.

Now read the first two readout lines together. Cadmium's geometric cross-section πR² is about 1.05 barns — Beiser quotes 1.06. Its absorption cross-section for thermal neutrons is 20,000. The nucleus is presenting a target nearly twenty thousand times its own size — which is nonsense for a particle and perfectly ordinary for a wave. A thermal neutron has a de Broglie wavelength of about 0.18 nm, some thirty thousand times the nuclear radius, so it is not a bullet being aimed at a target; it is a wave washing over a region vastly larger than the nucleus, and σ measures how strongly that region scatters it.

Switch to graphite and watch the curve go almost flat. Carbon barely absorbs neutrons at all — 0.0035 barns — while still being light enough to slow them down efficiently in elastic collisions. That combination is exactly what a moderator is, and it is why the first reactor Fermi built under the stands at Chicago was a pile of graphite bricks.

Select gold and set the flux to 1016.3 ≈ 2×1016. That is Example 12.10: a 10 mg foil reaching 200 µCi of 198Au takes about 410 seconds of exposure (at a flat 1016 it would be 820). Neutron-activation analysis works exactly this way, and it is sensitive enough to find parts per billion of an element in a sample without destroying it.

Why slow neutrons are the useful ones

A fast neutron has a short wavelength and behaves like a particle: its cross-section is roughly geometric. As it slows, its wavelength grows, the interaction region grows with it, and σ rises — typically as 1/v, which is why "thermal" neutrons at 0.025 eV are so much more reactive than the MeV neutrons that fission actually produces. Slowing neutrons down is therefore not a side effect in a reactor; it is the entire design problem, and the next card is about why.

The catch

A single number σ hides a great deal. Cross-sections depend strongly on energy and are not smooth: they have sharp resonances where the incident energy matches an excited state of the compound nucleus. 113Cd has one at 0.176 eV, and the 20,000 barns quoted here is the value at thermal energy near that resonance, not a constant. A reactor designer needs the whole curve, not a number.

Also, "cross-section" is always cross-section for a specified reaction. The figures here are for capture, except uranium's, which is for fission. The same nucleus has different cross-sections for scattering, capture, fission and everything else, and they add.

Look at the bottom chart. ²³⁵U + n releases about 7 MeV against a barrier of 5.7 — it fissions on a neutron of any energy at all, including one that has been slowed to room temperature. ²³⁸U + n releases only 5.6 MeV against a barrier of 6.2 — it falls short, and needs a neutron already carrying about an MeV of kinetic energy.

Where 200 MeV comes from

Straight off the binding-energy curve of Chapter 11. Uranium is bound at 7.6 MeV per nucleon and the fragments at about 8.5, so splitting 235 nucleons releases roughly 0.9 × 235 ≈ 200 MeV. About 83% of that appears immediately as kinetic energy of the two fragments flying apart under their own electrostatic repulsion; the rest arrives as neutrons, prompt gammas, and the beta decays of the fragments over the following minutes and years. That last part is why a shut-down reactor still needs cooling, and why spent fuel is a long-term problem.

The catch

The ε² expansion above is only the leading term, valid for small deformations. It tells you whether a nucleus is stable against a small stretch but cannot produce the actual barrier height, which requires the full Bohr–Wheeler treatment with higher-order shape terms — which is why the barriers on the lower chart are measured values rather than computed ones. The real fission path also passes through a doubly-humped barrier with a shape-isomeric well in between, a feature the liquid drop knows nothing about and which comes from shell effects in the deformed nucleus.

Fission is also not symmetric, which the liquid drop gets flatly wrong. It predicts a split down the middle; what actually happens is a lopsided division peaking near mass 95 and 140, with symmetric splits suppressed by a factor of several hundred. The asymmetry is a shell effect — the heavy fragment is pulled towards the doubly-magic 132Sn — and it took decades to explain.

Fusion: In Stars, and the Sixty-Year Attempt to Do It Here Secs. 12.11–12.12

The centre of the sun is fifteen million degrees, which sounds like a lot until you work out the Coulomb barrier between two protons. It is a thousand times too cold. Fusion happens anyway, and only because of tunnelling.

rate ∝ T−2/3e−3(EG/4kT)1/3,   EG = 2μc²(παZ1Z2)²

The problem, in one number

Two protons must approach to about 2 fm for the strong force to grab them. At that distance the Coulomb repulsion is around 0.7 MeV. At the centre of the sun, kT is 1.3 keV. The barrier is more than five hundred times the typical thermal energy, and the Maxwell–Boltzmann factor e−E/kT at that ratio is around 10−242. Classically the sun would be dark.

Two things rescue it, and the second plot shows them meeting. The Maxwell distribution has a tail, so a few pairs are much faster than typical. And those pairs do not need to clear the barrier — they can tunnel, exactly as the alpha particle did two cards ago, only inward. The tail is falling steeply with energy while the tunnelling probability is rising steeply, and their product is a narrow peak at an energy several times kT: the Gamow peak. Every fusion reaction in the universe happens inside that window.

Try this

Leave the star at 1.5×10⁷ K, the sun's core. On the first plot the proton–proton chain dominates. Drag up to 1.8×10⁷ K and the two curves cross; above that the CNO cycle takes over, which is why heavier, hotter stars burn hydrogen by a completely different route using carbon and nitrogen as catalysts.

Read the two exponents in the readout as you drag. At the sun's temperature the pp rate goes as about T4 and the CNO rate as about T20. Those are the textbook numbers, and neither was put in — they fall out of the Gamow energies, which depend only on the charges and reduced masses of the colliding nuclei. Nitrogen has seven protons, so its barrier is far higher, so its rate is far more temperature-sensitive. A CNO star is a hair-trigger; a pp star is a slow, stable furnace, and that stability is why the sun has managed four and a half billion years of near-constant output.

On the middle plot, note where the Gamow peak sits: several times kT, well out in the tail. Then note how narrow it is. The sun's power output is set by a sliver of its proton population that is neither typical nor extreme.

On the bottom plot, drag the reactor temperature and the confinement quality. The measured best from a tokamak — 30 keV and nτ = 2×1019 — is marked, and it sits just to the left of the breakeven curve: short by a factor of about 1.6. Sixty years of work, and the plasma still produces slightly less than it consumes. Ignition, where the alpha particles alone keep the reaction going, needs another factor of five on top.

Why the sun is slow, and why that is fortunate

The first step of the pp chain is p + p → d + e⁺ + ν, and it requires one of the two protons to beta-decay into a neutron during the collision. That is a weak-interaction process, and the weak interaction is feeble: the average proton in the sun's core waits about 1010 years for it. The sun's luminosity per unit mass is about a fifth of a human body's metabolic output. It is bright only because it is enormous — and it is long-lived precisely because the bottleneck reaction is so reluctant.

The catch

The rate curves here carry the correct temperature shape from the Gamow formula but not absolute magnitudes: the nuclear physics of each reaction enters through an S-factor, and the CNO rate also scales with a star's carbon and nitrogen content, so the crossing temperature depends on composition. The two curves have been normalised to cross at Beiser's 1.8×10⁷ K.

The D–T reactivity used for the Lawson plot is a standard fit to the Gamow form, good to about 25% below 25 keV. Above that it drifts, because the real D–T cross-section has a strong resonance and peaks near 65 keV. Real reactor design also has to account for bremsstrahlung losses, which rise with temperature and set a hard floor below which ignition is impossible in a hydrogen plasma at all.

One thing has changed since the book was written. Magnetic confinement still has not reached breakeven, but in December 2022 the National Ignition Facility got there by the other route — inertial confinement, 192 lasers on a peppercorn-sized capsule — releasing more fusion energy than the laser light delivered to the target. That is ignition in the sense defined above, though not yet in the sense of a power station: the lasers themselves consume about a hundred times more electricity than the shot produces.

Elementary Particles

Twelve chapters of this book were built on three particles. This one asks what those three are made of, and the answer turns out to be six quarks, six leptons, and a handful of particles whose only job is to carry forces between them. It is the deepest layer anyone has reached — and the chapter ends by pointing out how much of it is still guesswork.

Four Interactions, Thirty-Nine Decades Apart Sec. 13.1

Everything that has ever happened anywhere is one of four interactions doing its work. They differ in strength by a factor of 1039 and in range by a factor of 1018 — and which one matters depends entirely on how far apart you are looking.

Fem/Fgrav = ke²/Gm²    range = ℏc/mc²

What you're looking at

All four forces between two protons, as a function of how far apart they are, on a log-log plot spanning from well inside a nucleus out to a thousand atomic diameters. The point of the plot is the crossings. Inside about 1 fm the strong interaction is overwhelming. Beyond about 2 fm it has vanished entirely and the electromagnetic repulsion takes over. Gravity is a flat 1036 below the electric force at every separation and never catches up — and the weak interaction is off the bottom of the chart almost everywhere.

Try this

Read the electromagnetic-over-gravitational line. Between two protons it is 1.2 × 1036; between two electrons, 4.2 × 1042. Both forces fall as 1/r², so that ratio is a pure number — it does not depend on distance at all. Here is one way to feel how absurd it is. Take the hydrogen atom of Chapter 4 and replace the electrical attraction between proton and electron with their gravitational attraction, changing nothing else. The Bohr radius becomes ℏ²/Gme²mp, which works out to 1.2 × 1029 m — about 270 times the radius of the observable universe. A gravitationally bound atom would not fit in everything there is.

Then ask the obvious question: if gravity is 1036 times weaker, why is it the force that shapes the universe? Because it is the only one that cannot be cancelled. Matter in bulk is electrically neutral, so the electromagnetic force sums to nothing over a planet; the strong and weak forces die within a nucleus. Gravity has only one sign, so it adds up, and over a large enough pile of matter it wins by default. The four forces are not competing on the same field.

Slide to 10−15 m, about the size of a nucleon. The strong force is around 103 N — a hundred kilograms of weight, between two objects weighing 10−27 kg each. Now slide out to 10−14 m, ten times further. The strong force has fallen by a factor of 13,000, and it dropped below the electric repulsion somewhere around 4 fm. That one decade of distance is the whole reason nuclei have a maximum size: past a few fermis the binding stops arriving and only the repulsion is left.

Why the ranges are what they are

Chapter 11's Yukawa argument does all the work. An interaction carried by a particle of mass m has range ℏ/mc, so a massless carrier gives infinite range and a heavy one gives almost none. The photon is massless, so electromagnetism reaches across the universe. The W and Z weigh 80 and 91 GeV, so the weak interaction reaches 0.0025 fm — a thousandth of a proton, which is why it behaves as though it acts only on contact. The pion at 140 MeV gives 1.4 fm, which is the size of a nucleus. The one exception is the strong interaction between quarks: its carrier, the gluon, is massless and ought to have infinite range, but the force is short-ranged anyway for a completely different reason. That is the subject of the sixth card.

The catch

"Force" is the wrong language for the strong and weak interactions, and those two curves should be read as scale comparisons rather than as calculations. Both are drawn as the force from a Yukawa potential — exponential falloff with the range set by the mediator mass, 1.4 fm for the pion and 0.0025 fm for the W — normalised to 103 N at 1 fm, which is what 8 MeV of nuclear binding spread over 2 fm amounts to. The shapes are right and the crossings are in the right place; the absolute heights carry a normalisation, not a derivation. The electromagnetic and gravitational curves are exact.

The relative strengths quoted in the text (1, 10−2, 10−5, 10−39) are also not fixed constants. Coupling strengths run with energy: the electromagnetic coupling grows and the strong one shrinks as collisions get more violent, which is exactly why unification at very high energy is plausible at all.

A weak decay with almost no energy release is extraordinarily slow, and that slowness is why free neutrons survive long enough to be useful in reactors.

Finally, note the Ω⁻ at 1672 MeV. It is a baryon with three strange quarks, and it survives 10−11 s — an eternity for something that heavy. It cannot decay strongly, because that would need strangeness to change, and the strong interaction is forbidden to do that. It is stuck waiting for the weak interaction, which is the subject of the next card.

The catch

The book's Table 13.2 prints the tau's mean life as 2.9 × 10−23 s. That is a typographical slip — the tau lives 2.9 × 10−13 s, ten orders of magnitude longer, and it has to, because it decays weakly like the muon. The value used here is the correct one; the printed one would put the tau in the strong-decay band, which is impossible for a lepton that feels no strong force at all.

Neutrino masses are listed as "very small" and treated as zero throughout. They are not zero — oscillation experiments established that after the book was written — but they are below about 1 eV, some six orders of magnitude under the electron, so nothing on these plots changes.

Antimatter Sec. 13.2

Dirac's equation had solutions with negative energy, which looked like a fatal flaw. His way out of it — that all the negative states are already full, and a hole in them is a particle of opposite charge — predicted the positron four years before anyone saw one.

E = ±√(m²c⁴ + p²c²)    hν ≥ 2mc²

The problem Dirac had

The relativistic energy relation E² = m²c⁴ + p²c² has two roots, and there is no honest way to throw one away. But if negative energies down to −∞ are available, every electron in the universe should cascade into them and no atom would be stable. Dirac's answer was pure exclusion principle: all the negative states are occupied, one electron each, and Pauli forbids any more from joining. What we call the vacuum is a filled sea.

Then a photon of more than 2mc² can lift one electron out of the sea into a positive-energy state. What is left behind is a hole in the sea, and a hole in a sea of negative charge behaves in every respect like a particle of positive charge — exactly as a hole in a semiconductor valence band does, in Chapter 10. Dirac initially hoped the hole was the proton. It is the positron, and Anderson found it in cosmic rays in 1932.

Try this

With the electron–positron pair selected, drag the photon energy through 2. Below 1.022 MeV nothing happens at all — no partial promotion, no half a pair. Above it, a pair appears and the excess goes into their kinetic energy. This is the pair production of Chapter 2, seen from the other side.

Switch to proton–antiproton and read the threshold: 1877 MeV. That is why the antiproton was not found until 1955, twenty-three years after the positron — it took the Bevatron, a machine built more or less for the purpose, to reach the energy.

Now the lower chart. Annihilation releases c² = 9 × 1016 J per kilogram, which is 21.5 megatons of TNT — it converts all of the mass, where fission converts about a thousandth of it and chemistry about a billionth. Beiser's illustration is that a postage stamp of antimatter meeting a postage stamp of matter would put the space shuttle in orbit; at 50 mg each that is 9 × 1012 J against the shuttle's 3 × 1012 J of orbital kinetic energy, so the claim survives checking with a factor of three to spare.

The missing antimatter

Every particle has an antiparticle, the laws look almost perfectly symmetric between them, and the Big Bang should have made equal amounts. It did not — or if it did, essentially all of it annihilated. We see no annihilation gamma rays from the boundaries between matter and antimatter regions, and no antiparticles in the cosmic rays. The universe appears to be made entirely of one kind.

The best current account is that the decays of the heavy bosons in the first 10−35 s were very slightly asymmetric, leaving an excess of matter of perhaps one part in 30 billion. Everything else annihilated into photons, and those photons are the cosmic microwave background of the last card. Every atom in your body is a leftover from a rounding error.

The catch

The Dirac sea is a picture, not the modern theory, and it does not survive contact with bosons — there is no exclusion principle to fill a sea of negative-energy pions, yet antipions exist. Quantum field theory replaces it: particles and antiparticles are excitations of the same field, and neither is more fundamental. The sea is worth knowing because it is how the prediction was actually made, and because the hole analogy is genuinely the same mathematics as a hole in a semiconductor.

Note also that a lone photon in empty space cannot make a pair however energetic it is — momentum cannot be conserved. Pair production always happens near a nucleus, which takes up the recoil, and that is why the threshold quoted here is the lower bound rather than the exact requirement.

Is This Reaction Allowed? Sec. 13.4, Example 13.1

Particle physics has remarkably few rules, and they are all bookkeeping. Add up a handful of numbers before and after; if any of them changed, the reaction does not happen, no matter how much energy you throw at it.

Q, B, Le, Lμ, Lτ always conserved  |  S conserved except in weak decays, where ΔS = ±1

What you're looking at

A ledger. Pick particles going in and coming out, and every conserved quantity is summed on both sides. Nothing here is a judgement call: either the columns match or they do not, and the verdict at the bottom follows mechanically.

Try this

Start with n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ. Charge balances, baryon number balances (1 → 1 + 0 + 0), and electron number balances only because the antineutrino carries Le = −1 against the electron's +1. Now delete the antineutrino using the preset n → p + e⁻. Charge and baryon number are still fine, and the reaction still looks perfectly reasonable — but electron number goes 0 → 1 and the whole thing is forbidden. This is precisely the bookkeeping that forced the neutrino into existence.

Try μ⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄ₑ + ν_μ, Beiser's Example 13.1. Two different lepton numbers have to balance separately, and they do — the muon number is carried away by the muon-neutrino and the electron number by the pair. Then try replacing the ν̄ₑ with a ν_e and watch Le go to +2. The muon cannot simply turn into an electron; the generations are bookkept one by one.

Now the strangeness cases. Ξ⁻ → Λ⁰ + π⁻ changes S from −2 to −1: allowed, but only weakly, so it is slow — 10−10 s rather than 10−23. Then try Ξ⁻ → n + π⁻, which would change S by 2 in one step, and it is flatly forbidden. This is why the heavy strange baryons come apart in a cascade, one unit of strangeness at a time, rather than dropping straight to a nucleon — and it is why the Ξ was called the cascade particle before anyone knew why it behaved that way.

Try p + π⁻ → Λ⁰ + K⁰. Two strange particles appear together, with S = −1 and S = +1, summing to zero. Strange particles are always made in pairs in strong interactions, because the strong interaction cannot change the total strangeness — which was the observation that made Gell-Mann invent the quantum number in the first place.

Finally, p → e⁺ + π⁰. Charge balances, all three lepton numbers balance, energy is available, and the products are lighter. Only baryon number stops it: 1 → 0. That is the sole reason the proton is stable and therefore the sole reason atoms exist. Experiments have watched enormous tanks of water for decades and set a lower limit on the proton lifetime of about 1032 years, against a universe some 1010 years old.

Why conservation laws exist at all

Emmy Noether's theorem, proved in 1918, says that every conservation law corresponds to a symmetry. Invariance under translation in space gives conservation of momentum; under translation in time, conservation of energy; under rotation, angular momentum. Conservation of charge follows from a more abstract symmetry, gauge invariance. The laws in the ledger above — baryon number, the lepton numbers, strangeness — are believed to reflect symmetries of the same kind, though for some of them the symmetry is not yet identified, which is a polite way of saying we do not know why they hold.

The catch

Strangeness is the weakest of these rules and the ledger treats it accordingly. It is not a true conservation law — the weak interaction breaks it, by one unit at a time — and its real content is that the quark flavours can only be changed by one particular interaction. Baryon number is thought to be only approximately conserved too: every grand unified theory predicts proton decay, which is why those tanks of water are still being watched.

And a ledger that balances only tells you a reaction is not forbidden. It says nothing about the rate, which depends on which interaction mediates it and on the available phase space. A reaction that barely clears the energy threshold can be allowed and still essentially never happen.

It is one of the cleanest predictions in the history of physics, and it is visible on this plot as a straightedge exercise.

Why fractional charges were so hard to accept

Nothing else in nature has them. Every free particle ever observed carries a charge that is a whole multiple of e, and Millikan's oil-drop experiment had been finding exactly that since 1909. Gell-Mann himself was cautious about whether quarks were real objects or only a bookkeeping device. The evidence that settled it came from firing very high-energy electrons at protons, where the scattering pattern showed the charge inside a proton to be concentrated at three point-like centres — the same experiment Rutherford had done on the atom, one layer deeper.

The catch

The "constituent" quark masses in Beiser's Table 13.4 are effective values that already include most of the binding energy of the hadron, which is why simply adding them works at all. The current quark masses that appear in the underlying theory are far smaller — a few MeV for u and d, not 300 — and on that accounting more than 98% of the mass of a proton is not the mass of its quarks at all but the energy of the colour field holding them together. You are made almost entirely of binding energy.

The model as stated also has a glaring problem that the chapter mentions only in passing. The Ω⁻ is three strange quarks, all spin-up, all in the same state — a flat violation of the exclusion principle for identical fermions. The fix is colour: each quark carries one of three colour charges, so the three s quarks are not identical after all. Colour was invented to rescue the exclusion principle and turned out to be the charge of the strong interaction itself.

Why You Cannot Have One Quark Sec. 13.5

Every other force in physics gets weaker as things move apart. The colour force does the opposite — and that single reversed sign means a quark can never be taken out of a hadron, no matter how hard you pull.

V(r) = −(4/3)αsℏc/r + kr

What you're looking at

The potential energy between two quarks. At short range it looks ordinary — a 1/r attraction much like the Coulomb one, which is why quarks inside a hadron rattle around almost freely. At long range there is a second term that grows linearly with separation, as though the two quarks were joined by a spring that never yields. The colour field between them does not spread out the way an electric field does; it collapses into a narrow tube, and the energy stored in that tube is proportional to its length.

Try this

Read the string tension in newtons. At 1 GeV/fm it is about 1.6 × 105 N — sixteen tonnes of force, between two point particles, and it does not weaken with distance. This is the strongest force in nature by an enormous margin, and it is why the word "strong" was available.

Now drag the separation outward and watch the stored energy climb. At about 0.28 fm it crosses the line marked 2mπc² — the rest energy of two pions. Past that point, pulling the quarks further apart costs more energy than it takes to simply create a new quark–antiquark pair out of the vacuum, so that is what happens. The string snaps, and the two new quarks cap off the broken ends.

The result is worth sitting with. You started with one hadron and pulled on it. You end up with two hadrons, and never a free quark. Pull harder and you get more hadrons. Beiser's analogy is the right one: breaking a magnet in half does not give you an isolated north pole, it gives you two magnets. Try dragging the separation to 2 fm and read how many pairs the stored energy would buy.

Drop the string tension to 0.2 GeV/fm and watch the breaking distance stretch out. If the tension were small enough, quarks would separate freely and we would have found them in 1964. That we have not, after sixty years of looking, is the strongest evidence that the tension is real — and the theory is falsifiable in an unusual direction: finding a free quark would disprove quantum chromodynamics rather than confirm it.

Asymptotic freedom, the other half of the story

The short-range part of that curve is just as strange. Because the colour force strengthens with distance, it must weaken at short distance — and it does, to the point where quarks very close together behave almost as if free. This is why the electron-scattering experiments could see three sharp point-like charges inside a proton at all: at the short distances a high-energy electron probes, the quarks are barely interacting. The discovery that the strong coupling runs to zero at high energy, called asymptotic freedom, is what made a quantum field theory of the strong interaction possible, and it won the 2004 Nobel Prize.

The catch

The Cornell potential plotted here is a phenomenological form — it fits the observed spectra of heavy quark–antiquark bound states extremely well, and it is what lattice simulations of the theory produce, but it is not derived on this page. The string tension of about 1 GeV/fm and the coupling αs ≈ 0.3 are measured inputs.

The breaking condition is also a simplification. In practice a snapping string produces a spray of hadrons rather than one tidy pion pair, and what an experiment actually sees is a narrow jet of particles heading in the direction the quark was going. Jets are how quarks are observed: not as particles, but as the shrapnel of trying to remove one.

Field Bosons, and Why the Weak Force Looks Weak Sec. 13.6

The weak interaction is not intrinsically feeble. Its coupling is roughly the same size as electromagnetism's. It appears weak for one reason only — its carriers are eighty times heavier than a proton.

range = ℏc/mc²    strength ∝ [q²/(q² + MW²c⁴)]²

What you're looking at

Above: range against mediator mass, which is a single straight line because range × mass = ℏc = 197 MeV·fm, always. Every force carrier sits on that line. The photon and gluon are massless and so fall off the left-hand end at infinite range; the W and Z sit far to the right at 0.0025 fm.

Below: the weak interaction's strength relative to the electromagnetic one, as a function of collision energy. At the energies of beta decay it is down by twenty orders of magnitude. As the energy climbs it rises steeply, and at around 80 GeV — where a collision can finally produce a real W rather than a fleeting virtual one — the two curves meet.

Try this

Leave the energy at 1 MeV, the scale of nuclear beta decay. The weak interaction is about 10−20 of the electromagnetic, which is why beta decays take minutes rather than 10−16 seconds. Now drag the energy up and watch the gap close. By 10 GeV the suppression is only 10−4; by 200 GeV it is essentially gone.

This is what "unification" concretely means, and it is worth being precise about it. The two interactions do not become equal because one of them gets stronger. They were always the same strength. What changes is that at low energy the exchanged W has to borrow 80 GeV of rest energy it does not have, and the uncertainty principle only permits that for 10−26 s, which makes the exchange fantastically improbable. Give the collision 80 GeV and there is nothing to borrow. The weak force is electromagnetism with a heavy photon.

Read the W range: 0.0025 fm. Compare it with the pion's 1.4 fm and the proton's radius of about 0.8 fm. The weak interaction operates over a distance three hundred times smaller than a proton, which is as close to a contact interaction as physics gets, and it is why Fermi's original 1934 theory — which treated it as literally pointlike — worked so well for so long.

Where the masses come from

An electroweak theory with four massless carriers is mathematically beautiful and observably wrong: three of the four have to be heavy. The resolution is the Higgs field, which fills all of space and which the W and Z interact with strongly, acquiring what we measure as mass, while the photon does not interact with it at all and stays massless. The picture Beiser offers is a viscous drag, and the drag is what we call inertia. Since a field has a particle, the Higgs field has a Higgs boson, and finding it was the main reason for building the Large Hadron Collider.

The chapter was written while that machine was still being planned — Beiser writes that it "is planned to be operating in 2005" and that the Higgs mass "cannot be predicted, but is thought to be substantial, perhaps as much as 1 TeV". The LHC started up in 2008 and announced the Higgs boson on 4 July 2012, at 125 GeV — an eighth of the upper guess, and the last missing piece of the Standard Model.

The catch

The suppression curve is the propagator factor alone, which captures the energy dependence but not the couplings; a real calculation also carries the weak mixing angle and the running of both couplings with energy. The meeting point is genuinely near the W mass, but the two interactions are not described by a single coupling constant even above it — electroweak unification means they are two aspects of one structure, not that they become numerically identical.

The gluon is the awkward case for this whole way of thinking. It is massless, so the line above says its range should be infinite, and the strong interaction between quarks does in a sense have unlimited range — that is exactly what the previous card's linear potential says. What is short-ranged is the residual force between colour-neutral hadrons, which is a leakage effect carried by pions, in the same way that the van der Waals force between neutral atoms is a leakage of electromagnetism.

Three pieces of evidence

The Big Bang is not inferred from one observation. Three independent things point at it: the uniform expansion (Hubble's law, from Chapter 1), the helium abundance of 24% which no stellar process can account for, and the cosmic microwave background — a 2.7 K blackbody filling the sky in every direction, which is the light released when the universe cooled enough for atoms to form and went transparent. Those photons have been travelling unimpeded ever since, stretched by the expansion from visible light down to a millimetre wavelength. It is the oldest thing anyone has ever seen, and it is a better blackbody than anything ever built in a laboratory.

The catch

The thermal-history line is a single power law, which is right for the radiation era but not afterwards; once matter dominates, the exponent changes. The epoch markers are placed at their accepted times rather than derived.

And the fate plot is now known to be wrong in an important way. The chapter presents three futures set by the density alone, which is correct for a universe containing only matter. Since 1998 the expansion has been measured to be accelerating, which needs a fourth possibility the chapter does not draw: a universe with a large dark-energy component that expands forever and ever faster, regardless of its density. The current accounting is roughly 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, 68% dark energy — so the chapter's dark-matter discussion is about a quarter of the story, and the largest term in it had not yet been discovered when the book was written.
